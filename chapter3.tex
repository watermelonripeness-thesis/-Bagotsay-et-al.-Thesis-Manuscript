\chapter{METHODOLOGY}

{\baselineskip=2\baselineskip

This chapter describes the methodological framework used in developing the machine learning–based watermelon ripeness detection system. It details the research design adopted in the study, the requirements analysis, and the procedures for data gathering and acquisition. The chapter further explains the image collection process, data preprocessing techniques, model training and validation, and system evaluation methods. These procedures were systematically implemented to ensure accurate ripeness classification, model reliability, and consistency of results under real-world agricultural conditions.

\section{Research Design \& Procedure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption{The Modified Waterfall Model}
  \label{fig:the-modified-waterfall-model-244}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.927\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/the-modified-waterfall-model-32.png}}
\end{figure}

The study adopted the Modified Waterfall Methodology, which has a linear and sequential structure but with overlapping phases or feedback loops. This methodology is well suited for systems that combine hardware and software, making it a fit for this study.

The research design chosen for this study was the System Development Life Cycle (SDLC), specifically the modified Waterfall Model shown in Figure~\ref{fig:the-modified-waterfall-model-244} sequential structure of the Waterfall Model, which consists of requirements gathering, requirements analysis, system design and development, and testing, aligned with the implementation of image and audio classification for pre-harvest watermelons. Additionally, the modified Waterfall Model allowed the researchers to return to a previous phase whenever errors occurred in the current phase.

Because the core requirements of the system, such as the link between a watermelon’s acoustic response and its ripeness (\hyperlink{cite.wongkalasin2021}{Wongkalasin et al., 2021}; \hyperlink{cite.pamungkas2021}{Pamungkas \& Bintoro, 2021}) and the efficacy of machine learning classification and model fusion (\hyperlink{cite.arboleda2020}{Arboleda et al., 2020}; \hyperlink{cite.qi2024}{Qi et al., 2024}), were already well defined, a structured and sequential framework provided the necessary discipline for development. However, the modified aspect of this model was critical in addressing the transition from controlled laboratory conditions to the real-world field conditions targeted in this study, since the system deals with pre-harvest watermelons that are still attached to the vine.

By incorporating feedback loops between phases, this SDLC allowed for essential backtracking, which was particularly vital when integrating the acoustic sensor and image analysis, as both required iterative recalibration during the development and integration phases. Furthermore, the model accommodated the physical dependencies of the system itself. Audio data collection was gated by the successful design and integration of the pulse thumper, microphone, and microcontroller, since a consistent striking procedure was needed to record comparable thumps. Image data, on the other hand, was collected using smartphone cameras because the camera module had not yet been integrated into the prototype at the time of collection. This ensured that each stage, from hardware and software development to integration, underwent verification before progressing, mitigating the risk of cascading errors and giving the system the reliability needed to address the persistent issue of manual pre-harvest watermelon inspection in agricultural farms.

\section{Data Gathering}

The data for this study was gathered from two locations, a public market (Cogon Public Market, Cagayan de Oro City) on July 15, 2026, and a local farm (Brgy. Balubal, Cagayan de Oro City) on September 10, 2026. Ripe watermelon samples were collected at the market, while unripe samples were collected at the farm, where permission was secured from the farm owner to conduct the study within the premises during the pre-harvest season of watermelon. Watermelons sold at the market had already been harvested and confirmed ready for sale, while those at the farm were still in the field and had not yet reached maturity.

Two watermelon farmers were consulted through structured interviews, using a standardized interview guide consisting of closed- and open-ended questions, to provide insights regarding current practices for determining watermelon ripeness, common challenges encountered during the harvesting process, and accuracy concerns associated with existing manual assessment methods (\hyperref[app:section-G]{Appendix~G}). The first farmer, based at a farm in Brgy. Balubal, Cagayan de Oro City, also served as the source of the unripe samples for the study's data gathering. The second farmer, based at Bong Pelaez Farm, Zone 7, Mapawa, Malasag, Cugman, was consulted solely for additional insight, as their watermelon planting schedule falls later in the season and would not align with the study's data collection timeline. Both farmers raised similar concerns regarding the challenges and accuracy of manual assessment methods, though they differed in their primary ripeness cue: the first farmer relies on rind color, while the second relies on the spacing of the rind's stripes. Their firsthand experience in cultivating and harvesting watermelons allows the study to align the system design with real-world usage conditions, including the typical physical interaction with the fruit, environmental factors present in the field, and the practical expectations of agricultural workers regarding assessment speed and reliability.

The data gathering procedure involved two primary forms of data collection. Sound samples were collected by tapping the watermelons and recording the resulting acoustic responses using a prototype of the proposed device equipped with a microphone. Recordings of ripe watermelons were captured at the market, with ripeness confirmed by the vendors that the fruits were ready for sale, while recordings of unripe watermelons were captured at the farm, with their unripe state confirmed by the farm owner. In addition, images of both ripe and unripe watermelon rinds were captured using a Samsung Galaxy A51 and an iPhone XR at their respective collection sites.

All data collection procedures followed ethical considerations, including securing consent from the market vendors and written consent from the farm owner, ensuring that participation was voluntary, and maintaining the confidentiality of all collected information. The data was used solely for academic and research purposes to support the design, development, and evaluation of the proposed watermelon ripeness detection and classification system.

\section{Requirements Analysis}

This section defines the essential hardware and software specifications required to develop the watermelon ripeness detection device. By establishing the system’s architecture and functional framework, these requirements provide the technical blueprint necessary to guide the project from conceptual design to successful implementation.

\subsection{Hardware Requirements}

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Integrated Circuit.} The inclusion of a microcontroller or microprocessor is essential in the proposed system as it enables the integration and real-time execution of convolutional neural network, machine learning, and acoustic signal analysis within a single device. The system must sequentially process image data for watermelon color analysis and acoustic signals generated from tapping, extract relevant features from both modalities, and perform classification using trained machine learning models. These combined tasks require sufficient processing capability, including support for parallel execution, to handle multiple data streams efficiently. Local processing is critical in multimodal agricultural systems, as it enables synchronized handling of visual and acoustic data while maintaining real-time responsiveness and system autonomy (\hyperlink{cite.biglari2023}{Biglari \& Tang, 2023}).

\end{itemize}

Different microcontroller and microprocessor platforms present trade-offs in terms of processing speed, compatibility, peripheral support, and overall system performance. The Arduino Uno operates at a relatively low clock speed with limited memory, making it suitable for basic sensor interfacing but not for image processing or machine learning tasks. The ESP32 improves on this with a dual-core processor, higher clock speed, and support for communication protocols such as I2C, SPI, and UART, allowing better handling of multiple peripherals; however, it has limited native camera support. The ESP32-CAM integrates a camera module and supports wireless communication, making it compact and cost-effective, though its limited RAM constrains complex processing tasks. The ESP32-S3-CAM provides enhanced processing performance, improved memory capacity, and better support for AI-related instructions and camera interfaces, making it more suitable for handling both image and acoustic data, although it still operates under embedded resource constraints. In contrast, the Raspberry Pi 4 Model B and Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W offers significantly higher processing speed, larger memory, and compatibility with full operating systems and advanced libraries for neural networks and machine learning, but at the cost of higher power consumption and increased system complexity. These comparisons highlight the trade-offs between computational capability and resource efficiency, emphasizing the need to select a platform that balances performance, peripheral support, and energy consumption for reliable real-time operation (\hyperlink{cite.biglari2023}{Biglari \& Tang, 2023}; \hyperlink{cite.nugroho2024}{Nugroho et al., 2024}).

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Pulse} \textbf{Thumper.} A pulse thumper is essential for delivering a standardized, repeatable impact to the target surface. The thumper should be able to provide quick consistent force for each impact to ensure that the resulting acoustic signals are comparable across different measurements. It should be driven electronically, allowing for precise control over the impact force and timing. The thumper should also be adjustable to accommodate various testing conditions, ensuring the system’s versatility. Furthermore, it is important to minimize the risk of surface damage to the target during testing, ensuring the system is non-destructive while still producing accurate results.

\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Microphone.} A microphone is essential for recording the acoustic waves generated by a pulse thumper, as these signals possess frequency data directly linked to watermelon ripeness. The chosen sensor must be capable of precise recording within the target frequency spectrum and remain sensitive to minor resonance shifts. Strategic placement near the impact point is vital to filter out ambient interference and maintain steady data collection. Furthermore, digital output microphones are recommended to mitigate signal loss and noise, thereby enhancing the quality of the data used for analysis.

\end{itemize}

Different microphone categories offer various advantages regarding signal integrity, noise resistance, and hardware integration. Although analog electret condenser microphones are popular for their affordability and ease of use, they are prone to interference and necessitate extra circuitry for signal conditioning. Conversely, digital MEMS sensors, including the INMP441 and SPH0645LM4H, feature integrated analog-to-digital converters and utilize I2S communication to provide higher noise immunity and clearer data. The MAX9814 Electret Microphone Amplifier offers a middle ground with automatic gain control to stabilize signal levels, though this may interfere with features reliant on precise amplitude measurements. Existing research suggests that MEMS microphones are better suited for embedded acoustic tasks because of their small footprint, reliable frequency stability, and resistance to external noise, whereas analog versions are still practical for budget-restricted or basic setups (\hyperlink{cite.zhang2023}{Zhang \& Li, 2023}). These factors underscore the importance of choosing a microphone that optimizes the trade-off between signal precision, stability, and hardware compatibility.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Camera.} A small camera unit is necessary to photograph the watermelon’s exterior for ripeness assessment, specifically to analyze visual indicators like texture and skin color. The device must deliver adequate resolution and color fidelity under ambient outdoor light to facilitate dependable feature extraction. Positioned on the housing to face the specimen, the camera needs to interface seamlessly with the main processor while minimizing energy draw for mobile operation.

\end{itemize}

Various camera modules offer different balances of image fidelity, computational needs, and hardware compatibility. Entry-level options, such as the OV7670, provide basic imaging but lack integrated compression, which places a heavier burden on the processor. Alternatively, the OV2640 module offers improved resolution and hardware-based JPEG compression, effectively lowering memory and processing overhead. The ESP32-CAM provides a highly integrated, affordable alternative by combining a microcontroller with a camera sensor (usually the OV2640) and wireless connectivity; however, its restricted memory and processing power may limit the complexity of image analysis compared to more robust platforms. Research in embedded vision emphasizes that choosing a camera requires weighing image quality against power and processing constraints to ensure stable, real-time functionality in field settings (\hyperlink{cite.cao2025}{Cao et al., 2025}; \hyperlink{cite.awais2025}{Awais et al., 2025}). These considerations highlight the importance of selecting a module that efficiently meets the system’s operational goals.

\item \textbf{Power Supply.} The device must be powered by a reliable, rechargeable battery that can support all system components during typical field use. The battery should be selected based on the power requirements of the microcontroller and mechanical actuator, providing enough capacity for the system to operate without frequent recharging. \end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Justification of Selected Hardware Components}

The selection of hardware components for the proposed watermelon ripeness detection system is guided by the requirements of multimodal data acquisition, real-time embedded processing, energy efficiency, and system reliability within resource-constrained environments. The system integrates acoustic analysis and image-based classification; therefore, each component was chosen based on its ability to provide accurate sensing while operating within the memory, processing, and power limitations of an embedded platform.

The ESP32-S3-N16R8 was selected as the primary microcontroller unit (MCU) to serve as both the real-time peripheral controller and the on-device machine learning inference engine. Powered by a dual-core Xtensa LX7 processor operating at up to 240 MHz with integrated vector (SIMD) instructions and hardware floating-point support, the ESP32-S3 bridges the computational gap between conventional low-power microcontrollers and power-hungry single-board computers (\hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis, 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}).

A critical hardware constraint in embedded TinyML systems is the internal memory ceiling (\hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}). Conventional microcontrollers restricted to 256–512 KB of on-chip static random-access memory (SRAM) encounter a severe memory bottleneck once real-time operating system (FreeRTOS) tasks, peripheral drivers, and communication stacks are initialized (\hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}). As demonstrated by \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk and Biriukov (2026)}, system and driver overhead on the ESP32-S3 reserves approximately 150 KB of the 512 KB internal SRAM, leaving less than 360 KB of usable on-chip memory—an allocation insufficient to simultaneously store deep neural network weights, intermediate activation tensors, and high-resolution input buffers. Selecting the N16R8 variant directly resolves this limitation by pairing the SoC with 16 MB of Quad-SPI Flash and 8 MB of Octal-SPI (OPI) pseudo-static RAM (PSRAM) (\hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis, 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}). In embedded vision pipelines on the ESP32-S3, \hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis (2026)} showed that pre-allocating a 172 KB RGB working buffer (\(240 \times 240 \times 3\)) alongside convolutional feature maps (\textasciitilde{}235 KB total inference footprint) inside the 8 MB Octal PSRAM prevents internal SRAM exhaustion and eliminates dynamic heap fragmentation while consuming under 3\% of available PSRAM. Similarly, \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk and Biriukov (2026)} confirmed that offloading higher-resolution (\(96 \times 96\)) spatial activation tensors to the 8 MB PSRAM enables depthwise-separable convolutional architectures to execute reliably on the ESP32-S3, while the 16 MB Flash accommodates memory-mapped model parameters and firmware assets without crowding internal SRAM.

Beyond memory capacity, the ESP32-S3 is uniquely suited for hybrid edge-AI architectures that combine deep computer vision backbones with lightweight classical classifiers on a single chip (\hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis, 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.moura2026}{Moura et al., 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}). \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk and Biriukov (2026)} demonstrated that 8-bit integer (INT8) post-training quantization on the Xtensa LX7 core preserves classification accuracy within 0.15 to 1.0 percentage points of full-precision (float32) baselines across both standard convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and depthwise-separable MobileNet architectures, yielding deterministic, bit-exact inference. Furthermore, \hyperlink{cite.moura2026}{Moura et al. (2026)} benchmarked multi-paradigm machine learning models on an ESP32-S3 at 240 MHz and established that INT8 quantized convolutional models running on TensorFlow Lite Micro (TFLM) execute within tens of milliseconds using a bounded PSRAM tensor arena (\(\leq 192KiB\)), whereas native C/C++ Decision Trees execute in 3.68 to 4.65 \ensuremath{\mu}s with zero PSRAM arena overhead and 0.04 \ensuremath{\mu}s of net inference energy. These findings directly validate deploying both a quantized convolutional vision model and a rapid native Decision Tree classifier concurrently on the ESP32-S3-N16R8 (\hyperlink{cite.moura2026}{Moura et al., 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}).

The ESP32-S3-N16R8 also satisfies the deterministic timing, multimodal interfacing, and portable power requirements of a standalone field prototype (\hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis, 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.moura2026}{Moura et al., 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}). Its rich peripheral matrix—supporting parallel DVP camera capture, I2S digital audio input, high-speed SPI display output, I2C, and hardware PWM/GPIO control—allows simultaneous sensor acquisition and electromechanical actuation without an auxiliary co-processor (\hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis, 2026}; \hyperlink{cite.moura2026}{Moura et al., 2026}). \hyperlink{cite.moura2026}{Moura et al. (2026)} verified that the ESP32-S3 sustains deterministic, index-synchronized sensor sampling over serial buses alongside microsecond-level hardware timing (esp\_\allowbreak{}timer\_\allowbreak{}get\_\allowbreak{}time()), while \hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis (2026)} demonstrated real-time \(240 \times 240\) camera frame capture, lookup-table resizing, CNN inference, and display rendering at 6.3 FPS (\(159ms/frame\)). Across empirical hardware benchmarks, the ESP32-S3 maintains a sub-watt active power envelope—drawing approximately \(0.37W\) during standalone compute (\hyperlink{cite.myroshnyk2026}{Myroshnyk \& Biriukov, 2026}) and \(0.40 - 0.50W\) (\(120 - 150mA\) at \(3.3V\)) during active camera capture and vision inference (\hyperlink{cite.ellis2026}{Ellis, 2026}), making it well suited for battery-powered, handheld deployment.

The OV5640 camera module is selected for image acquisition due to its high-resolution imaging capability and onboard image signal processing (ISP). Compared to lower-resolution alternatives such as the OV7670 and OV2640, the OV5640 provides improved detail through its 5-megapixel resolution, enabling more accurate extraction of visual features such as color variation and surface texture associated with ripeness. The module’s built-in JPEG compression reduces memory and bandwidth requirements, while ISP functions such as auto-exposure and auto white balance partially offload low-level image processing from the microcontroller. However, consistent with its documented characteristics, the OV5640 still imposes moderate processing and integration requirements, including driver configuration and memory management. Despite its relatively higher power consumption, its superior image quality and processing support justify its selection for CNN-based classification tasks.

The INMP441 MEMS microphone is chosen to support acoustic analysis due to its digital I2S output and stable signal characteristics. Unlike analog microphones that rely on analog-to-digital conversion and may introduce noise or distortion, the INMP441 directly outputs 24-bit PCM digital audio, ensuring higher signal integrity. This allows the system to capture subtle variations in resonance and damping properties produced during the thumping process. Additionally, the absence of automatic gain control (AGC) preserves the consistency of amplitude-related features, which is critical for machine learning-based classification. Although its frequency response is limited to approximately 60 Hz to 15 kHz, this range remains sufficient to capture the dominant acoustic signatures relevant to watermelon ripeness detection.

A 6V push-pull solenoid will be employed to generate controlled acoustic excitation. This actuator provides direct linear motion, producing consistent and repeatable impacts on the watermelon surface. Such repeatability is essential to ensure that variations in recorded acoustic signals are attributable to differences in fruit properties rather than inconsistencies in the excitation mechanism. The solenoid’s simple ON/OFF control also facilitates precise timing synchronization with the microphone using minimal control logic. While the device exhibits moderate durability and relatively higher power consumption compared to alternatives such as servo-based or motor-driven mechanisms, it remains more suitable for this application due to its simplicity, strong impulse force, and high repeatability under intermittent operation conditions.

For user interaction, an IPS TFT LCD display with an I2C interface will be incorporated to display camera output, classification results, and system status. The module provides a simple and resource-efficient output method, requiring minimal processing overhead and only two communication lines, thereby preserving available GPIO pins. In comparison to OLED displays, the LCD offers better visibility under strong ambient lighting conditions, making it more appropriate for outdoor or agricultural environments where readability is critical. Although limited to character-based output, its functionality is sufficient for the system’s requirements.

The system will be powered by two 3.6V 18650 lithium-ion batteries in series, selected for its high energy capacity, cost-effectiveness, and widespread availability. This battery type supports the system’s portability and is capable of supplying the combined power demands of the microcontroller, camera module, microphone, display, and solenoid actuator. Its standardized cylindrical form factor enables easy replacement and maintenance in field conditions. While the use of this battery requires an external protection circuit or battery management system, this is a standard design consideration and does not significantly increase system complexity.

The selected components collectively address the functional and non-functional requirements of the system while explicitly accounting for embedded resource constraints. The integration of high-quality sensing devices, a capable yet energy-efficient processing unit, and a reliable actuation mechanism enables accurate acquisition and analysis of both acoustic and visual data. This design ensures that the system can perform real-time ripeness classification in a portable and practical manner suitable for field deployment.

\subsection{Software Requirements}

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Programming Language and IDE.} The firmware and on-device processing logic were written in C++ using the Arduino IDE, which has strong community support for the ESP32-S3 and its audio and camera libraries. Python was used for dataset preparation, feature extraction, and model training, using librosa for audio processing, scikit-learn and XGBoost for classifier training, and TensorFlow with Keras for the image models. The trained models were then converted into formats compatible with the microcontroller for on-device inference.

\item \textbf{Signal Processing.} The signal processing component extracted acoustic features from the recorded thump signal to identify patterns that correlate with watermelon ripeness. Each recording was first segmented to isolate the thump response, then transformed from the time domain into the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The resulting spectrum was mapped onto the Mel scale, log-compressed, and passed through a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) to produce Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), which, together with spectral and time-domain features, formed the feature set for audio classification. Frequency-domain and cepstral analysis techniques are commonly used for feature extraction in audio-based classification tasks (\hyperlink{cite.zhang2023}{Zhang \& Li, 2023}). Feature extraction was prototyped in Python using the librosa library, which was used to process the full dataset and generate the features for model training. For on-device processing, the same pipeline was reimplemented in C++ on the ESP32-S3. The Mel filterbank, DCT basis, and feature scaling parameters were precomputed in Python and exported as header files. The exported DCT basis was checked against librosa’s implementation and agreed to within about 10\textsuperscript{-}\textsuperscript{6}, and the exported Decision Tree produced the same predictions as the Python model on all 99 test recordings when run on a computer.

\item \textbf{Deep Learning.} The image processing component analyzed captured watermelon rind images, which served as inputs to a deep learning–based classification model. The implementation of such models requires careful consideration of resource constraints, including memory usage, computational complexity, and accuracy, which can significantly influence system performance and feasibility (\hyperlink{cite.biglari2023}{Biglari \& Tang, 2023}; \hyperlink{cite.zhang2023}{Zhang \& Li, 2023}). Consequently, multiple deep learning architectures were evaluated and tested to ensure that the selected model maintained acceptable classification performance while remaining computationally efficient. Without this evaluation, there would be a risk of selecting a model that operates inefficiently or fails to meet practical implementation requirements.

\item \textbf{Acoustic Classification.} The ripeness of the watermelon was determined from the acoustic pipeline using a trained machine learning classifier, which classified the 31 standardized acoustic features of each thump as ripe or unripe. The selected Decision Tree was converted into C code and deployed directly on the microcontroller, allowing it to run without requiring a separate inference framework. For result fusion, the outputs of the acoustic classifier and the deep learning–based image classifier were cross-validated through log-likelihood ratio fusion, in which each prediction is weighted according to the reliability of its classifier, producing a final classification of ripe or unripe, or an inconclusive result when the two classifiers disagree without a clear decision.

\item \textbf{Version Control.} Git was used to manage the project’s source code and track changes throughout development. A shared repository on GitHub allowed the team to collaborate and maintain a history of the project as it evolved.

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{User Definition}

The proposed watermelon ripeness detection and classification system is intended for individuals directly involved in pre-harvest assessment of watermelons in agricultural settings. The primary user of the system is the end user, while the microcontroller serves as the secondary system actor responsible for processing sensor inputs and generating the required classification outputs. Defining these users and actors is necessary to clarify how the software responds to acoustic input, visual input, and output presentation during field operation.

The end user refers to watermelon farmers and agricultural workers who use the device in the field to assess the ripeness of watermelons prior to harvest. This user initiates the ripeness assessment by capturing an image of the watermelon rind, then holding the device against the watermelon surface so that the thumper can tap the fruit and produce an acoustic response. The end user receives the ripeness classification result through the TFT LCD display and uses this information to make informed harvesting decisions. As the main intended beneficiary of the system, the end user interacts with it through physical handling of the device, initiation of the image capture and tapping procedure, and interpretation of the classification result.

The microcontroller serves as the secondary actor of the system because it is responsible for carrying out the core processing operations required for ripeness detection and classification. It manages the capture of acoustic signals through the microphone, the acquisition of visual data through the camera module, the extraction of acoustic features through MFCC-based signal processing, the classification of those features, the analysis of rind color through deep learning, the fusion of the acoustic and visual classification results through log-likelihood ratio fusion, and the presentation of the final result to the end user. The microcontroller functions as the central processing component that enables the system to respond to physical inputs and produce the corresponding classification outputs defined in the use case diagram.

At the center of the system is the ESP32-S3 N16N8 WROOM1 CAM microcontroller, which serves as the brain or the hub for processing both audio and image analysis. Key components include the INMP441 microphone, OV5640 camera, solenoid, and ST7789 TFT IPS display.

Based on the datasheet of the ESP32-S3-WROOM CAM, the built-in camera port shares the same connections as the left GPIO pins of the ESP32-S3 board except GPIO 14, which the OV5640 camera will use. GPIO pins 15, 16, 17, 18, 8, 19, 20, and 3 of the MCU is the 8-bit parallel bus, which are grouped to allow the ESP32-S3's LCD-CAM peripheral to "read" the state of all 8 pins at once in a single clock cycle. Pin 46 (PCLK) of the MCU tells the ESP32 when a new pixel of data is ready, pin 6 (XCLK) provides a high-frequency (usually 20MHz or 24MHz) clock to "drive" the camera's internal brain, pins 7 \& 15 (HSYNC/VSYNC) signal the end of a line (Horizontal) and the end of a frame (Vertical). They are essential for the ESP32 to "reconstruct" the image in memory, and pin 45 (PWDN) is used to put the camera to sleep. The microcontroller is powered by two rechargeable 18650 batteries through a stable 5V step down buck converter with its output pins connected to the 5V and GND pins of the ESP32-S3. The batteries are connected to a BMS module for protection and a 2S charger with a USB Type-C port for charging. Two kinds of switches are connected to the microcontroller, one connected to the 5V rail to turn the system on and off, and a pushbutton connected to GPIO45 to trigger the camera then both the solenoid and microphone.

The INMP441 uses I2S to stream digital audio with precise timing directly to the microcontroller. Pin 25 (GPIO42/SCK) connects to the microphone’s SCK to provide the serial clock, pin 26 (GPIO41/WS) connects to WS to select between left and right channels, and pin 24 (GPIO2/SD) connects to SD for digital audio data transfer, with the L/R pin tied to GND to configure left-channel output. The ST7789 TFT IPS display uses an SPI interface to communicate with the microcontroller, utilizing pins GPIO1 for DC, GPIO38 for RST, GPIO40 for SDA, and GPIO39 for SCK.

The push-pull solenoid, which is responsible for thumping the watermelon, is connected to a single channel relay module. A relay is used because the solenoid is a high power component, 6V and a maximum peak of 2A of current is used during thump, to which the GPIO pins of the ESP32-S3 alone cannot supply. The input pin of the relay is connected to GPIO19, which will drive the relay coils and in turn activate the solenoid. The solenoid is connected to the BMS, which is powered by the batteries along with a flyback diode, preventing high voltage and current spikes from frying the microcontroller.

\paragraph{Context Level Diagram}

The context level diagram presents the proposed watermelon ripeness detection system as a single high-level process that interacts with two main external entities, namely the farmer and the microcontroller, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:context-level-diagram-452}.

The farmer initializes the system, which starts the system and sets the sensors, thumper, and LCD display to their default values. The farmer also receives the visual output from the system through the LCD display when initializing the camera and after the image and audio models have finished classifying.

The microcontroller, on the other hand, receives the raw image capture from the system and applies preprocessing on the captured image in order to extract the appropriate features from the watermelon rind. \paragraph{Data Flow Diagram}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption{Data Flow Diagram}
  \label{fig:data-flow-diagram-475}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.000\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/data-flow-diagram-61.png}}
\end{figure}

The Data Flow Diagram represents the internal flow of data when the external entities, namely the farmer and microcontroller, interact with the system and its data storage as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:data-flow-diagram-475} flow of data begins when the farmer initializes the system, which sets the values of all sensors, the thumper, and the LCD display to their default. This sends a signal to the microcontroller to start the image capture for the raw image, which is first displayed live in the LCD then preprocessed. The raw image is first color corrected, which ensures consistent and color-accurate representation regardless of lighting conditions, and then passed through an RGB to HSV conversion process to yield the HSV values of the image. The HSV values are then used for feature extraction, which generates an image feature vector which is then put through an image classification process, which determines whether the image is of a ripe or unripe watermelon based on its features. The resulting image ripeness classification is then stored in the Image Ripeness Data storage.

After image classification, a signal is sent to the thumper, and the resulting audio is recorded by the microcontroller. The raw audio signal is first passed through thump detection, which locates the peak of the thump response and isolates a short window around it, discarding the surrounding silence and handling noise. The windowed thump is then divided into frames with a Hann window to reduce discontinuities at the frame edges and prevent spectral leakage. A Fast Fourier Transform is then applied to each frame, converting it from a time-domain signal into its frequency components.

From these frequency components, spectral centroid, bandwidth, and rolloff are computed, while zero-crossing rate and RMS energy are computed directly from the waveform. The frequency components are also mapped onto the Mel scale, log-compressed, and passed through a Discrete Cosine Transform to produce 13 Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), whose mean and standard deviation are taken across the frames. Together, these form a 31-feature vector, which is standardized using the scaling parameters obtained during training. The audio classification process then uses this feature vector to produce an audio ripeness classification, which is stored in the Audio Ripeness Data Storage.

Once both classifications are available, the decision fusion process retrieves the image and audio ripeness classifications, together with their confidence levels, from their respective data stores. The two classifications are cross-validated through log-likelihood ratio fusion, in which each is weighted according to the reliability of its classifier, producing a final result of ripe or unripe, or an inconclusive result when the two classifiers disagree without a clear decision. This final result is stored in the Final Ripeness Data Storage. Finally, the ripeness result display process retrieves the final result from storage and displays it to the farmer through the system’s LCD screen to complete the data flow of the system.

\subsubsection{Pipeline}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption{System Pipeline}
  \label{fig:system-pipeline-556}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.886\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/system-pipeline-65.png}}

The model development pipeline begins with two datasets, one of rind images and one of audio recordings of the thump, which are each prepared separately. Preprocessing and feature engineering turn the raw data into inputs that the models can learn from. The images are standardized to a uniform size, checked for duplicates, and divided into training, validation and test sets. The audio recordings are converted into numerical features. The prepared data is then passed to two independent training branches. In the image branch, lightweight convolutional neural network architectures are trained to classify the rind as ripe or unripe. In the audio branch, machine learning classifiers are trained on the extracted acoustic features. Within each branch, the candidate models are compared against one another on held-out data. The best-performing model from each modality is then selected and exported for deployment, where the two models serve as the visual and acoustic components of the fused classification described in Section 3.4.3.

\subsection{System Integration}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption{System Integration}
  \label{fig:system-integration-561}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.000\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/system-integration-64.png}}
\end{figure}

The system operates through two sequential sensing modalities that together characterize the ripeness of a watermelon, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system-integration-561}. The acoustic modality processes the sound data captured from a mechanical thump on the fruit’s surface using digital signal processing, while the visual modality analyzes the appearance of the rind through a convolutional neural network. The outputs of both modalities are then cross-validated through log-likelihood ratio (LLR) fusion to produce a final classification of ripe or unripe, which is displayed to the user, or an inconclusive result when the two classifiers disagree without a clear decision.

In multimodal systems, classifiers trained on different types of data can be combined at the decision level, where each classifier first makes its own prediction and only these predictions are combined. One such approach is the naive Bayes combination, in which each classifier’s vote is weighted by its reliability as measured from its confusion matrix, under the assumption that the classifiers make independent errors (\hyperlink{cite.kuncheva2001}{Kuncheva et al., 2001}). In agricultural applications, decision-level fusion is commonly used when data from different sources are too distinct to be combined at an earlier stage, with a separate classifier applied to each source before their outputs are combined (\hyperlink{cite.barbedo2022}{Barbedo, 2022}).

This approach suited the present study, since the image and audio datasets were collected separately and were not paired by individual watermelon, so the two classifiers could not be combined by a model trained on both. The naive Bayes combination requires only each classifier’s true and false positive rates, and it works on the binary output of the deployed decision tree, which cannot provide a probability for averaging. It also allows the more reliable classifier to override the other when they disagree, and produces a probability of ripeness that lets the system report an inconclusive result rather than risk a false positive, the costlier error for a non-climacteric fruit. Since these rates are computed in advance, the fusion requires only a few additions and a comparison at run time, making it suitable for the ESP32-S3.

Taking the logarithm of the naive Bayes combination converts each classifier’s prediction into a weight that reflects how reliable that classifier has been, known as its log-likelihood ratio (\hyperlink{cite.kuncheva2001}{Kuncheva et al., 2001}). When a classifier predicts ripe, its weight is the logarithm of its true positive rate (TPR) divided by its false positive rate (FPR), as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:formula-566}, and when it predicts unripe, its weight is the logarithm of (1 \ensuremath{-} TPR) divided by (1 \ensuremath{-} FPR), as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:formula-567}. The TPR is the proportion of ripe watermelons the classifier correctly labels as ripe, while the FPR is the proportion of unripe watermelons it incorrectly labels as ripe. A small constant \ensuremath{\epsilon} of 0.001 is added to each rate to avoid division by zero, and the subscript m denotes either the image or the audio classifier.

\begin{equation}
  LLR_{m}\  = \ ln(\frac{TPR_{m} + \ \epsilon}{FPR_{m} + \ \epsilon})
  \label{eq:formula-566}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  LLR_{m}\  = \ ln(\frac{1 - \ TPR_{m}\  + \ \epsilon}{1 - FPR_{m}\  + \ \epsilon})
  \label{eq:formula-567}
\end{equation}

The TPR and FPR are selected according to how reliable each classifier’s prediction is expected to be. For the image classifier, one pair of rates is used when its confidence is at least 0.85 and another when it is lower. For the audio classifier, one pair is used when the thump window is detected adaptively and another when it is not and a fallback window is used instead. A separate pair for weak decision margins was also defined in the firmware but is never selected, since the exported Decision Tree outputs only a class label without a confidence score. This allows a more reliable prediction to carry more weight than a less reliable one.

The weights of the two classifiers are then added to a prior log-odds term L\textsubscript{0} to give the combined log-odds L, as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:formula-570}, which is converted into a probability of ripeness using the logistic function in Equation~\eqref{eq:formula-571}. The prior log-odds is set to 0, so that neither class is favored before any evidence is considered. Adding the two weights assumes that the image and audio predictions are independent given the true ripeness of the watermelon (\hyperlink{cite.kuncheva2001}{Kuncheva et al., 2001}), so that each prediction provides separate evidence.

\begin{equation}
  L\  = \ L_{0} + LLR_{image}\  + \ LLR_{audio}
  \label{eq:formula-570}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  P(ripe)\  = \ \frac{1}{1 + e^{- L}}
  \label{eq:formula-571}
\end{equation}

The watermelon is classified as ripe when L is greater than 0.40 and as unripe when L is less than \ensuremath{-}0.40. When L falls between \ensuremath{-}0.40 and 0.40, corresponding to a probability of ripeness between about 0.40 and 0.60, the system reports an inconclusive result and the user is prompted to repeat the assessment. When the two classifiers disagree, the one with the larger weight determines the final classification, so that a more reliable prediction can override a less reliable one.

\subsubsection{System Architecture}

The system architecture utilizes a multimodal processing framework that integrates visual and acoustic data through a synchronized three-stage pipeline. By leveraging a central microprocessor to orchestrate sensor inputs, the design ensures seamless communication between the imaging system and the acoustic sensors, creating a robust foundation for automated watermelon ripeness detection.

  \label{fig:system-architecture-576}

The operational flow begins with image classification through a convolutional neural network, which triggers a mechanical pulse, followed by the capture and processing of acoustic signals through cepstral and spectral feature extraction and classification. The two classifications are then cross-validated through LLR fusion, as described in Section 3.4.3, to determine the final result, which is communicated to the user through the integrated LCD interface.

\section{System Testing}

Field testing was conducted at a watermelon farm in Barangay Balubal, Cagayan de Oro City. Each watermelon was assessed with the handheld prototype, which captured a rind image at approximately 10 cm and was then placed against the fruit for tapping and acoustic recording. The same fruit was also assessed by an experienced farm harvester and then cut open, with the internal flesh inspection serving as the ground truth. In total, 46 assessments were recorded on 43 watermelons, comprising 35 ripe and 8 unripe watermelons according to the destructive inspection.

Ripe and Unripe were the two prediction classes. An Inconclusive result was coded as an assessment error and prompted a retrial on the same fruit. The three Inconclusive results were excluded from the binary confusion matrix, which used the retrial result for each affected fruit, but they remained errors in the first-attempt evaluation.

Watermelons selected for testing were assessed by the system and subsequently evaluated by experienced farm harvesters to establish a ground truth reference for each sample. The system was operated in its intended handheld configuration, with each watermelon tested individually by holding the device at a 10 cm distance from the watermelon to capture an image of the watermelon fruit rind, and then placing the device against the fruit to tap using the thumper and record the generated audio. Multiple trials were performed per sample to ensure consistency and reliability of results.

Testing was conducted in a single session under open-field conditions, with natural lighting and ambient background noise. For each assessment, the individual predictions of the acoustic and image classifiers were recorded alongside the fused result, so that each could be evaluated separately.

Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score form the core of the classification evaluation and work together to describe how well the system distinguishes between unripe and ripe watermelons. Accuracy provides the most straightforward view by measuring the overall proportion of samples the system classifies correctly, though it can be misleading on its own when one class appears more frequently than others. Precision and recall address this limitation by reporting performance at the individual class level. Precision captures how often the system's positive predictions for a given class are actually correct, essentially measuring how much the system avoids false alarms, while recall captures how many of the true instances of each class the system successfully identifies, measuring how much it avoids misses. The F1-score then combines both into a single balanced figure per class, which is particularly important here because ripe watermelons are expected to be more prevalent in field conditions, meaning a high overall accuracy score could still mask poor detection of the less common unripe category.

While the metrics above summarize performance numerically, the confusion matrix presents the full picture of classification outcomes by showing every combination of predicted versus actual ripeness class in a single table. This makes it possible to see not just how often errors occur, but what kind of errors they are, specifically, whether the system more frequently misclassifies unripe watermelons as ripe or ripe watermelons as unripe, which have different practical consequences for harvest decisions. Beyond evaluating the fused system, the confusion matrix is also used to cross-validate the classifications produced by the acoustic and visual subsystems against each other, revealing whether both pipelines tend to agree on their predictions or whether one modality consistently produces different outputs than the other. Per-class precision, recall, and F1-score extend this by reporting those figures separately for each of the two ripeness categories rather than relying solely on overall averages, ensuring that strong performance on the dominant class does not obscure weaker performance on the other.

The Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC) is computed independently for the acoustic subsystem and the visual subsystem rather than for the fused system alone. Unlike accuracy or F1-score, which are tied to a specific decision threshold, ROC-AUC evaluates each modality's ability to separate the two classes across the full range of possible thresholds. This makes it a more robust indicator of discriminative ability and is particularly useful for determining which of the two pipelines (the thumping sound analysis or the image-based inspection) contributes more strongly to the final ripeness prediction before they are combined.

Cohen's Kappa coefficient compares the system's classifications against the ground truth labels provided by experienced farm harvesters, but unlike raw accuracy it accounts for the probability that some of that agreement occurs by chance rather than through genuine system performance. This gives a more honest measure of how much the system truly agrees with expert judgment, rather than simply how often their outputs happen to match, making it a more meaningful indicator of real-world reliability against a human reference standard.

The System Usability Scale (SUS) is a reliable and widely validated tool for measuring perceived usability across non-technical user groups (\hyperlink{cite.brooke1996}{Brooke, 1996}), making it appropriate for assessing a handheld agricultural device intended for users with limited technical background. It was administered as a standardized ten-question Likert-scale survey after users interact with the system during testing. SUS scores range from 0 to 100 and were interpreted using the adjective rating scale where scores around 85.5 correspond to Excellent, 71.4 to Good, 50.9 to OK, 35.7 to Poor, and 20.3 to Awful (\hyperlink{cite.bangor2009}{Bangor et al., 2009}). Inference time complemented this by measuring how long the system takes to process sensor inputs and return a ripeness classification, confirming that the device operates quickly enough for real-time use during harvest without creating delays that would make it impractical.

The Signal-to-Noise Ratio is measured specifically for the acoustic pipeline to evaluate whether the thumping audio captured by the INMP441 microphone is clean enough to support reliable feature extraction in an outdoor farm setting. Because the device is intended for use in real field conditions, the audio signal is exposed to background noise from wind, farm machinery, and general ambient activity, all of which can interfere with the acoustic features the system relies on. A sufficient SNR confirms that the microphone and signal processing setup can isolate the meaningful thumping response from that environmental noise before classification even begins.

Finally, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient measures trial consistency by evaluating whether the system produces stable and repeatable outputs when the same watermelon is tested multiple times under the same conditions. This is a direct reliability requirement, since a system that gives different ripeness classifications across repeated trials on the same sample cannot be trusted regardless of how well it performs in single-trial accuracy tests. Across all of these metrics, results are also reported separately for the acoustic subsystem and the visual subsystem before being reported for the fused system, which allows the contribution of each pipeline to be assessed on its own terms and confirms that combining both modalities produces a meaningful improvement over relying on either one alone.

\subsection{Accuracy}

\begin{equation}
  Accuracy\  = \ \frac{TP\  + \ TN}{N}\ x\ 100\%
  \label{eq:formula-599}
\end{equation}

Accuracy measures the percentage of assessments that agree with the verified ripeness of the watermelon. In the equation, TP and TN are the correct positive and negative predictions, and N is the total number of assessments being evaluated. The definition follows \hyperlink{cite.fawcett2006}{Fawcett (2006)}.

For field evaluation, accuracy was calculated for the original tests and again for the final result of each fruit after the recorded re-trials. An original inconclusive output remained in the denominator and was counted as an error. Each fruit was counted once in the final evaluation. This shows how often the device gave a correct answer at each stage.

\subsection{Precision}

\begin{equation}
  Precision\  = \ \frac{TP}{TP\  + \ FP}\ x\ 100\%
  \label{eq:formula-603}
\end{equation}

Precision measures how many predictions for a class are correct. TP represents correct predictions for the class being measured, while FP represents other samples incorrectly assigned to that class. The formula follows \hyperlink{cite.fawcett2006}{Fawcett (2006)}.

For RIPE precision, the measure shows how often a watermelon labelled RIPE was actually ripe when opened. A higher value means fewer unripe fruits were incorrectly labelled RIPE. Precision was interpreted together with recall because correct RIPE answers alone do not show whether some ripe fruits were missed.

\subsection{Recall}

\begin{equation}
  Recall\  = \ \frac{TP}{TP\  + \ FN}\ x\ 100\%
  \label{eq:formula-607}
\end{equation}

Recall measures how many samples that truly belong to a class are correctly identified. TP represents the samples correctly found, while FN represents samples from that class that received the other label. The definition follows \hyperlink{cite.fawcett2006}{Fawcett (2006)}.

For RIPE recall, the measure shows how many verified ripe watermelons the device identified as RIPE. A higher recall means fewer ripe fruits were labelled UNRIPE. Reporting recall for each class makes the direction of the errors easier to understand.

\subsection{F1 Score}

\begin{equation}
  F1\  = \ \frac{2TP}{2TP\  + \ FP\  + \ FN}\ x\ 100\%
  \label{eq:formula-612}
\end{equation}

The F1 score combines precision and recall into one measure. It decreases when either false positive or false negative predictions increase. The count-based formula is equivalent to the harmonic mean of precision and recall described by \hyperlink{cite.fawcett2006}{Fawcett (2006)}.

In this study, F1 was used to describe the balance between giving correct predictions for a ripeness class and finding the samples that belonged to that class. It was also used when selecting the audio-model settings. The class being evaluated was identified in each result so the score could be interpreted consistently.

\subsection{Confusion Matrix}

\begin{equation}
  Row\ percentage\  = \frac{C_{ij}}{n_{i}}\ x\ 100\%
  \label{eq:formula-616}
\end{equation}

A confusion matrix places the reference classes in rows and the predicted classes in columns. Its diagonal cells contain correct answers, while the other cells contain errors. The binary matrix has two rows and two columns for RIPE and UNRIPE. In the equation, C\textsubscript{ij} is the count in row i and column j, and n\textsubscript{i} is the total for reference class i.

Count matrices show how many watermelons were correctly identified or given the wrong label. Row percentages show the same outcomes relative to the number of samples in each class. The final field matrix uses the recorded re-trial answer for each original inconclusive output; the original inconclusive errors are reported separately.

\subsection{Per Class Measures and Macro Averages}

\begin{equation}
  P_{macro\ } = \ \frac{P_{R} + P_{U}}{2}
  \label{eq:formula-621}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  R_{macro} = \frac{R_{R} + R_{U}}{2}
  \label{eq:formula-622}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  {F1}_{macro\ } = \ \frac{{F1}_{R} + {F1}_{U}}{2}
  \label{eq:formula-623}
\end{equation}

Precision, recall, and F1 can be calculated separately for each class by treating that class as the positive class. A macro average then gives the two class scores equal weight (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-c). Here, P means precision and R means recall. The subscripts R and U identify the RIPE and UNRIPE classes, respectively.

The image-model comparisons report both class results and macro averages. The audio notebook reports RIPE as its positive class, and the field summaries also identify their reported class. These labels distinguish a score for one class from an average across both classes.

\subsection{ROC-AUC}

\begin{equation}
  ROC\ AUC\  = \ \int_{0}^{1}{}TPR(u)\ du
  \label{eq:formula-627}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  TPR\  = \ \frac{TP}{TP\  + \ FN}
  \label{eq:formula-628}
\end{equation}

The receiver operating characteristic curve shows how the true positive rate (TPR) changes with the false positive rate (FPR) as the decision cutoff is varied. ROC-AUC is the area under that curve. In the integral, u represents FPR. A value closer to one indicates stronger separation of the two classes.

ROC-AUC was used to examine the completed image and audio models on their respective reserved test sets. The image curves use UNRIPE as the positive class, while the audio curves use RIPE. The reference labels and prediction scores use the same positive class within each calculation. The curves are presented in Figures~\ref{fig:roc-curves-of-the-selected-image-configurations-1146} and~\ref{fig:roc-curves-of-the-tuned-acoustic-classifiers-1050}.

\subsection{Inference and Processing Time}

\begin{equation}
  Mean\ time\  = \ \frac{t_{1} + t_{2} + ... + t_{n}}{n}
  \label{eq:formula-632}
\end{equation}

Processing time measures how long a recorded routine takes to complete. Its arithmetic mean is the sum of the measured durations divided by their number. In the equation, t\textsubscript{1} to t\textsubscript{n} are recorded durations expressed in the same unit, and n is the number of timing records.

The notebook inference times describe model execution in the notebook environment. The device image timer includes image preparation, resizing, classification, and diagnostic output, while its sound timer covers audio analysis and classification. These defined intervals show which routine contributes most to processing delay. Image capture, tap recording, and user handling occur outside the displayed device timings.

\subsection{Signal-to-Noise Ratio}

\begin{equation}
  {SNR}_{dB} = 10\log_{10\ }(\frac{P_{w}}{P_{b}})
  \label{eq:formula-636}
\end{equation}

Signal-to-noise ratio expresses the strength of a sound relative to background noise. P\textsubscript{w} was the mean squared amplitude in the selected tap window, and P\textsubscript{b} was the corresponding power measured from the background segment before the tap. The result is expressed in decibels (dB).

Because the tap window also contains background sound, the notebook value is a window-to-background estimate. A higher value indicates a more prominent tap relative to its background. This measure supported the audio-noise analysis and the selection of noise levels used when creating augmented training recordings.

\subsection{Cohen’s Kappa}

\begin{equation}
  \kappa_{dB} = \frac{{p\ }_{o} - {\ p\ }_{e}}{1\  - \ {p\ }_{e}}
  \label{eq:formula-640}
\end{equation}

Cohen’s Kappa measures the level of agreement between two raters, correcting for the probability that agreement occurred purely by chance. Unlike a simple percentage accuracy, which can be artificially inflated if one class is highly dominant (e.g., if most watermelons are ripe), Kappa provides a more robust statistical measure of true reliability. The metric ranges from \ensuremath{-}1 (complete disagreement) to +1 (perfect agreement), with a value of 0 indicating agreement no better than random chance.

The alternating positive and negative statements prevent automatic responses and yield a composite score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate better usability, while lower scores suggest significant usability issues.

\section{Data Set Preparation}

This section presents the preparation of the image and audio datasets used for watermelon ripeness classification. The process covers the collection and labeling of the samples, the analysis of each dataset, the processing applied to the images and recordings, and the augmentation of the training data. The prepared image dataset was used to train eight convolutional neural network architectures, while the audio dataset was used to train seven machine learning classifiers.

\subsection{Data Collection}

The datasets used for both the visual and audio components of the system were collected by the researchers from a local market and a farm in Cagayan de Oro City, and all samples were of the Sweet 16 variety. Ripe watermelon samples were gathered on July 15, 2026, from Cogon Public Market, where the watermelons had already been harvested and were sampled while resting on the vendors’ display surfaces. For the image component, ripe watermelon rind images were captured using a Samsung Galaxy A51 and an iPhone XR, since the camera module had not yet been integrated into the prototype, while for the audio component, tapping sound recordings were collected using the pulse thumper prototype to ensure a consistent striking procedure. Ripe ground truth labels were established based on the vendors’ confirmation that the watermelons were ready for sale, rather than through physical cutting or internal inspection of the fruit.

Unripe watermelon image and audio samples were collected on September 10, 2026, in the field at a farm in Purok 4, Brgy. Balubal, about 7 days before the farm’s first scheduled harvest, while the watermelons were still attached to the vine and resting on the soil. The unripe state of the watermelons was confirmed by the farmer.

For both classes, multiple recordings were taken from each watermelon, and the images and recordings were collected independently, so individual fruits were not tracked and the image and audio samples were not paired by fruit. Since the two classes were collected at different sites, on different dates, and in different fruit positions, differences between them may partly reflect these collection conditions, which is examined in Section 4.2.1.4. All samples were organized and stored according to their assigned binary label prior to preprocessing.

\subsection{Audio Preprocessing}

This subsection describes how the raw recordings were converted into the acoustic features used to train the classifiers. It also presents an exploratory analysis of these features to examine how they related to each other and to the ripeness label.

\textbf{Thump Window Detection}

All recordings were captured at a sampling rate of 16 kHz as single-channel (mono) audio. Each recording lasted about 1.3 to 1.4 seconds, but the thump response occupied only a short portion of it, with the rest consisting of near-silence, background sound, and handling noise. Computing features over the full recording would therefore mostly describe this noise instead of the fruit’s acoustic response, so the thump response was first isolated using an adaptive windowing method.

The adaptive window was built in two steps: locating the thump peak, then finding where the response ended. To avoid noise from handling the device, the first 0.3 seconds of each recording were excluded before searching for the peak. Within the remaining signal, the frame with the highest root mean square (RMS) energy was located using 15 ms frames with a 5 ms hop, and the sample with the highest absolute amplitude within that frame was taken as the thump peak. The window began 20 ms before the peak and ended where the smoothed RMS envelope stayed more than 20 dB below the peak amplitude for at least 30 ms, up to a maximum length of 0.6 seconds.

All 494 recordings produced a valid thump window. Sample recordings are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sample-recordings-with-detected-thump-windows-700} for (a) ripe and (b) unripe watermelons, with the detected window shaded on each waveform. The trimmed segments of the same recordings, which were used for feature extraction, are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:trimmed-thump-segments-used-for-feature-extracti-705} for (a) ripe and (b) unripe watermelons. Each segment begins with a short stretch of near-silence before the impact, followed by the oscillating decay of the thump.

\textbf{Dominant Frequency Analysis}

To compare the recordings with the dominant frequencies reported in the literature, the dominant frequency of each recording was taken as the frequency with the highest FFT magnitude, using a Hann window and zero-padding to 16,384 points. The detected thump windows were too short for this purpose, since 16 ripe and 49 unripe recordings had their peak fall at the 50 Hz lower limit of the search. A longer segment, from 20 ms before the thump peak to 300 ms after it, was used instead, where only one unripe recording reached this limit. The dominant frequency was found in two ranges: 50 to 400 Hz, which covers the frequencies reported by \hyperlink{cite.pamungkas2021}{Pamungkas and Bintoro (2021)}, and 50 to 2,000 Hz, a wider range that also includes the higher frequencies of the thump.

\subsection{Image Preprocessing}

The watermelon images were cleaned through central cropping, resizing, glare reduction, sharpening, and lighting and color normalization. Each image was cropped to a central square whose side length corresponded to 70\% of the shorter original dimension, reducing peripheral background while retaining the central rind region. The cropped images were resized to 224 \ensuremath{\times} 224 pixels using area interpolation for downsampling and bicubic interpolation for upsampling. Glare was identified in the HSV color space using a value threshold of at least 240 and a saturation threshold of at most 30. The detected regions were expanded using a 5 \ensuremath{\times} 5 kernel and reconstructed through Telea inpainting with a radius of five pixels. A 3 \ensuremath{\times} 3 sharpening filter was then applied to emphasize local edges and rind patterns.

Lighting and color normalization were performed using OpenCV’s 8-bit CIELAB representation. Common reference values were calculated from the median lightness and mean color-channel values of the images across both classes. The resulting references were 183.00 for the lightness channel, 116.74 for the a channel, and 151.79 for the b channel. Each image’s lightness channel was shifted to align its median with the lightness reference, while the a and b channels were shifted to align their means with the corresponding color references. Adjusted values were clipped to the range of 0–255. These references were calculated before dataset partitioning. The preprocessing stage produced images at 224 \ensuremath{\times} 224 pixels, which were subsequently resized to 96 \ensuremath{\times} 96 pixels when loaded for model training.

\subsection{Audio Augmentation}

Audio augmentation was applied to the training recordings using NumPy and librosa, prior to feature scaling, hyperparameter tuning, and model training. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of each recording was computed as the ratio of the thump window’s power to that of the background segment before the thump, expressed in decibels. The ripe recordings, collected at the market, had a median SNR of 29.5 dB within the thump window, while the unripe recordings, collected at the farm, had a median of 54.0 dB, so neither set reflected the louder background noise the device may encounter in open fields. Additive background noise was therefore used to improve the generalization of the classifiers to noisier field conditions, since it changes the recording environment without altering the frequency content of the thump that separates ripe from unripe samples. Each training recording was given two augmented copies, one per noise type described below, while the test set was left unchanged.

\textbf{Selection of Noise Type and Level}

To select the noise types and levels, a sensitivity test was conducted on the training recordings using white, pink, brown, and real background noise at SNRs of 30, 20, 10, and 5 dB. The share of noisy copies assigned to the wrong class by a reference classifier trained on the clean data was measured, along with the length of the detected thump windows. At 10 dB and below, the windows lengthened for all noise types, reaching the 0.6-second limit for white and pink noise, and the misclassification rate rose sharply, with 30\% to 41\% of the ripe copies misclassified even under real and brown noise. White and pink noise also shifted the spectral features toward the opposite class at higher SNRs, while real and brown noise kept the misclassification rate near that of the clean data at 20 and 30 dB, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:misclassification-rate-of-noisy-training-copies-758} and Figure~\ref{fig:noise-sensitivity-of-classification-and-window-d-760}. Real and brown noise were therefore selected, each added at an SNR drawn at random between 20 and 30 dB relative to the power of the clean thump window.

\subsection{Image Augmentation}

Image augmentation was applied to the training set to improve the model’s ability to generalize to variations in image orientation during actual field use. The implemented transformations consisted of random horizontal flipping, random vertical flipping, and random rotation. These transformations were applied dynamically during training, producing variations of the existing images without creating a fixed additional set of saved files. The original ripeness labels were retained, and no random augmentation was applied to the validation and test sets.

\textbf{Horizontal and Vertical Flip}

Horizontal and vertical flipping reversed the training images from left to right and from top to bottom, respectively, while preserving their original ripeness labels. These transformations exposed the model to different orientations of the same rind features. They were intended to reduce the model’s dependence on a fixed arrangement of visual patterns during classification.

\section{Model Training, Validation, and Testing}

This section describes how the classification models for both modalities were developed and evaluated. Seven machine learning classifiers were trained on the extracted acoustic features, while eight convolutional neural network architectures were trained on the prepared rind images. The section first describes each classifier and architecture, then presents the training procedure for each modality, including hyperparameter tuning, followed by the validation process used to select the final configurations and the testing procedure used to evaluate them on data not used during training.

\subsection{Classifiers \& Architectures}

This subsection enumerates the classifiers used for audio classification and the neural network architectures used for image classification in this study. Each classifier and architecture is described in terms of how it works and the reason for its inclusion in this study. These descriptions provide the basis for the training, validation, and testing procedures discussed in the succeeding subsections.

\subsubsection{Classifiers}

Seven supervised machine learning classifiers were trained and compared for the binary classification of the extracted acoustic features: Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machine, Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, and K-Nearest Neighbors. These algorithms represent different learning approaches, namely probabilistic, margin-based, tree-based, ensemble, linear, and instance-based learning, which allowed the study to determine which type of decision boundary best separated ripe from unripe acoustic responses. Because the selected model had to run on the ESP32-S3, the comparison also considered the size of each trained model and whether it could be converted for on-device inference. The following subsections describe each classifier and the hyperparameters tuned for it.

\textbf{Naive Bayes}

Naive Bayes is a probabilistic classifier based on Bayes' theorem. It assumes that the input features are conditionally independent of one another given the class (\hyperlink{cite.domingos1997}{Domingos \& Pazzani, 1997}). For each sample, the classifier computes the posterior probability of each class and assigns the class with the highest probability. Naive Bayes was included as a lightweight probabilistic baseline for comparison with the other classifiers. Its inclusion is consistent with previous research on watermelon quality classification, in which Naive Bayes was evaluated alongside other supervised learning algorithms using acoustic signals and image-derived features (\hyperlink{cite.chawgien2021}{Chawgien \& Kiattisin, 2021}).

This study used the Gaussian variant, which models each feature within each class as a normal distribution with a mean and variance estimated from the training data. The tuned hyperparameter was var\_\allowbreak{}smoothing, which adds a portion of the largest feature variance to all variances to keep the calculation numerically stable.

\textbf{Support Vector Machine}

The Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a supervised classifier that constructs a decision boundary to separate samples belonging to different classes (\hyperlink{cite.cortes1995}{Cortes \& Vapnik, 1995}). Through a kernel function, the input vectors could be represented in a higher-dimensional feature space, allowing the classifier to construct linear or nonlinear decision boundaries. The formulation also accommodated training data that could not be perfectly separated. SVM was included because it was suitable for evaluating classification performance on a relatively small, high-dimensional dataset consisting of 31 acoustic features and 494 recordings. Its application was also supported by previous studies involving acoustic watermelon classification and maturity prediction (\hyperlink{cite.chawgien2021}{Chawgien \& Kiattisin, 2021}; \hyperlink{cite.koc2025}{Koç \& Akbalik, 2025}). The different kernel options allowed the study to assess whether the acoustic responses of ripe and unripe watermelons were better separated by a linear or nonlinear decision boundary.

The tuned hyperparameters were the regularization parameter C, the kernel type (linear, radial basis function, polynomial, or sigmoid), the kernel coefficient gamma, the polynomial degree, the independent kernel term coef0, and the class weighting. Gamma applies to the radial basis function, polynomial, and sigmoid kernels. The degree applies only to the polynomial kernel, and coef0 applies only to the polynomial and sigmoid kernels. The balanced class weighting adjusts the weight of each class in inverse proportion to its frequency in the training data. The classifier was configured to output class probability estimates.

\textbf{Decision Tree}

The Decision Tree classifier used in this study follows the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) method (\hyperlink{cite.breiman1984}{Breiman et al., 1984}). The tree recursively partitions the feature space using binary splits. At each node, it selects the feature and threshold that minimize the impurity of the resulting child nodes, and splitting continues until a stopping condition is reached. Each terminal node, or leaf, assigns a class, and the predicted class probability is the fraction of training samples of that class within the leaf.

The Decision Tree classifier was included because of its interpretability, as its decision rules provided a means of examining which acoustic features contributed to the distinction between ripe and unripe watermelons (\hyperlink{cite.maindonald2024}{Maindonald et al., 2024}). Its structure of feature thresholds and branching rules also made it a candidate for lightweight on-device inference. However, its suitability for deployment on the ESP32-S3 depended on the measured model size, memory requirements, and inference time.

The tuned hyperparameters were the maximum tree depth, the minimum number of samples required to split a node, the minimum number of samples required at a leaf, the impurity criterion (Gini impurity or entropy), the number of features considered at each split, and the class weighting.

\textbf{Random Forest}

The Random Forest classifier is an ensemble of decision trees in which each tree depends on a random vector sampled independently and with the same distribution for all trees (\hyperlink{cite.breiman2001}{Breiman, 2001}). Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the training data when bootstrapping is enabled, or on the full training set otherwise. Only a random subset of features is considered when splitting each node. The predictions of all trees are combined to produce the final classification.

Random Forest was included to evaluate whether combining multiple decision trees improved classification performance and reduced the variability associated with an individual tree. This was particularly relevant given the limited number of acoustic recordings. The classifier also provided feature importance scores and supported class weighting to address differences in the number of ripe and unripe samples. Its inclusion was further supported by previous acoustic fruit-classification studies. Random Forest achieved high classification accuracy in research on pineapple maturity and defects (\hyperlink{cite.arwatchananukul2024}{Arwatchananukul et al., 2024}) and was among the classifiers evaluated for predicting watermelon maturity from hand-hitting sounds (\hyperlink{cite.koc2025}{Koç \& Akbalik, 2025}).

The tuned hyperparameters were the number of trees, the maximum tree depth, the minimum number of samples required to split a node and at a leaf, the number of features considered at each split, whether bootstrap sampling was used, and the class weighting. The balanced\_\allowbreak{}subsample option computes the class weights separately from the bootstrap sample of each tree (\hyperlink{cite.scikit2026}{scikit-learn developers, 2026}).

\textbf{XGBoost}

XGBoost is a gradient tree boosting system that predicts the output as the sum of the outputs of several regression trees (\hyperlink{cite.chen2016}{Chen \& Guestrin, 2016}). Trees are added one at a time, and each new tree is fitted to reduce a regularized objective made up of a training loss and a penalty on model complexity. The penalty includes a term on the number of leaves, controlled by gamma, and an L2 term on the leaf weights, controlled by reg\_\allowbreak{}lambda. The contribution of each new tree is scaled by a shrinkage factor, set through the learning rate, and features can be subsampled when building the trees.

XGBoost was also included because it offered a boosting-based approach for learning from tabular acoustic features, including Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs). Its inclusion also allowed the study to compare sequential boosting with the tree aggregation approach used by Random Forest. Previous benchmark research demonstrated that tree-based methods, including gradient-boosting approaches, remained competitive for tabular datasets (\hyperlink{cite.grinsztajn2022}{Grinsztajn et al., 2022}). Furthermore, gradient-boosted trees achieved the highest classification accuracy among eight classifiers in a watermelon sweetness classification study that combined acoustic signals and image processing (\hyperlink{cite.chawgien2021}{Chawgien \& Kiattisin, 2021}). These findings provided a basis for evaluating XGBoost on the study's own ripe and unripe watermelon dataset.

The tuned hyperparameters were the number of trees, the maximum tree depth, the learning rate, the row subsampling ratio, the column subsampling ratios per tree and per level, the minimum sum of instance weight required in a child node, gamma, and the L1 (reg\_\allowbreak{}alpha) and L2 (reg\_\allowbreak{}lambda) regularization terms. Binary log loss was used as the evaluation metric.

\textbf{Logistic Regression}

Logistic Regression models the probability that a sample belongs to the positive class as a logistic function of a weighted linear combination of its features (\hyperlink{cite.cox1958}{Cox, 1958}). A regularization penalty is added during training to limit the size of the weights. The L1 penalty uses the absolute values of the weights, the L2 penalty uses their squared values, and the elastic net penalty combines both (\hyperlink{cite.zou2005}{Zou \& Hastie, 2005}). It was included as a linear baseline to determine whether the extracted acoustic features could distinguish ripe from unripe watermelons using a linear decision boundary. Its regularization options also provided a means of controlling model complexity when dealing with potentially correlated MFCC features. Because its predictions were based on a weighted combination of the input features, the resulting model was also evaluated as a potential candidate for resource-constrained microcontroller deployment. Its practical suitability depended on the measured inference time and memory requirements on the target device.

The tuned hyperparameters were the inverse regularization strength C, the penalty type, the solver, the elastic net mixing ratio l1\_\allowbreak{}ratio, and the class weighting. The l1\_\allowbreak{}ratio value sets the proportion of the L1 penalty and is used only with the elastic net penalty. The liblinear solver supports the L1 and L2 penalties, while the saga solver also supports the elastic net penalty. The maximum number of solver iterations was set to 5,000.

\textbf{K-Nearest Neighbors}

The K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier assigns a sample to the class most frequently represented among its (k) nearest training samples (\hyperlink{cite.cover1967}{Cover \& Hart, 1967}). The nearest samples are identified using a selected distance metric. KNN was included as a non-parametric, instance-based approach that did not require a predefined linear or parametric decision boundary. This allowed the study to evaluate whether ripe and unripe acoustic responses formed distinguishable local clusters within the feature space.

The inclusion of KNN was also supported by previous research on watermelon maturity prediction using hand-hitting sounds, in which KNN was among the classifiers evaluated (\hyperlink{cite.koc2025}{Koç \& Akbalik, 2025}). However, unlike classifiers that store a compact set of learned parameters, KNN required access to the training samples during prediction. Consequently, the storage requirements associated with retaining the training dataset were considered when evaluating its suitability for deployment on the ESP32-S3.

The tuned hyperparameters were the number of neighbors (k), neighbor weighting, distance metric, Minkowski power parameter (p), and leaf size. Neighbor contributions were weighted either uniformly or inversely according to their distances. The distance metrics evaluated were Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski, and Chebyshev. The power parameter (p) applied only to the Minkowski metric, while the leaf size controlled the size of the tree structure used for neighbor searching.

\subsubsection{Architectures}

\textbf{MobileNetV1}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[MobileNetV1 architecture. Adapted from Phiphiphatphaisit and Surinta (2020).]{MobileNetV1 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.phip2020}{Phiphiphatphaisit and Surinta (2020)}.}
  \label{fig:mobilenetv1-architecture-adapted-from-phiphitpha-822}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.636\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/mobilenetv1-architecture-adapted-from-phiphitphatphaisit-and-88.png}}
\end{figure}

MobileNetV1 is built on depthwise separable convolutions, which split a standard convolution into a depthwise convolution that filters each channel separately and a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 pointwise convolution that combines the channels (\hyperlink{cite.howard2017}{Howard et al., 2017}). The network begins with a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 convolution followed by 13 depthwise separable blocks. Each block applies a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 depthwise convolution and a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 pointwise convolution, with batch normalization and ReLU6 after each, and the network has no skip connections. A width multiplier thins every layer uniformly. This study used a multiplier of 0.25, which gives 8 filters in the stem and pointwise layers of 16, 32, 32, 64, 64, 128 (blocks 6 to 11), 256 and 256. Stride-2 depthwise convolutions in blocks 2, 4, 6 and 12 reduce the feature map from 48\ensuremath{\times}48 to 3\ensuremath{\times}3, so the backbone outputs 3\ensuremath{\times}3\ensuremath{\times}256 features.

The ImageNet weights were frozen and the input was scaled to the range \ensuremath{-}1 to 1. The backbone has 218,544 parameters, and the dense layer adds 257 trainable parameters.

\textbf{MobileNetV2}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[MobileNetV2 architecture. Adapted from Muhathir et al. (2026).]{MobileNetV2 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.muhathir2026}{Muhathir et al. (2026)}.}
  \label{fig:mobilenetv2-architecture-adapted-from-muhathir-e-827}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.636\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/mobilenetv2-architecture-adapted-from-muhathir-et-al-2026-89.png}}
\end{figure}

MobileNetV2 replaces the blocks of its predecessor with inverted residual blocks and linear bottlenecks (\hyperlink{cite.sandler2018}{Sandler et al., 2018}). Each block expands the channels with a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution by a factor of six, filters them with a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 depthwise convolution, and projects them back to a narrow representation with a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution that has no activation. The input is added to the output when the stride is 1 and the channel counts match. ReLU6 follows the expansion and depthwise layers. The network has a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 stem of 32 filters, 17 blocks, and a final 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution to 1280 channels. The first block has no expansion and produces 16 channels. The remaining blocks form six stages with 24, 32, 64, 96, 160 and 320 output channels, containing 2, 3, 4, 3, 3 and 1 blocks. Stride-2 convolutions at the start of the 24-, 32-, 64- and 160-channel stages reduce the feature map to 3\ensuremath{\times}3. The backbone outputs 3\ensuremath{\times}3\ensuremath{\times}1280 features, and ten of its blocks have skip connections.

This study used the full-width model (width multiplier 1.0) with frozen ImageNet weights and the input scaled to the range \ensuremath{-}1 to 1. The backbone has 2,257,984 parameters, and the dense layer adds 1,281 trainable parameters.

\textbf{MobileNetV3}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[MobileNetV3 architecture. Adapted from Qian et al. (2021).]{MobileNetV3 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.qian2021}{Qian et al. (2021)}.}
  \label{fig:mobilenetv3-architecture-adapted-from-qian-et-al-833}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.636\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/mobilenetv3-architecture-adapted-from-qian-et-al-2021-90.png}}
\end{figure}

MobileNetV3 was designed with hardware-aware architecture search and combines the inverted residual blocks of MobileNetV2 with squeeze-and-excitation modules and the hard-swish activation (\hyperlink{cite.howard2019}{Howard et al., 2019}). This study used the minimalistic variant of the Small model, which removes squeeze-and-excitation and hard-swish and uses ReLU with 3\ensuremath{\times}3 depthwise kernels only. The network has a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 stem of 16 filters, 11 inverted residual blocks, and a final 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution to 576 channels. The first block has no expansion and a stride of 2. The next ten blocks have expansion sizes of 72, 88, 96, 240, 240, 120, 144, 288, 576 and 576 channels and output 24, 24, 40, 40, 40, 48, 48, 96, 96 and 96 channels. Blocks 1, 3 and 8 use stride 2, so the feature map is reduced to 3\ensuremath{\times}3, and six blocks have skip connections.

The ImageNet weights were frozen, and the scaling of the input to the range \ensuremath{-}1 to 1 is part of the Keras model. The backbone has 441,000 parameters, and the dense layer adds 577 trainable parameters.

\textbf{EfficientNet-B0}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[EfficientNet-B0 architecture. Adapted from Su and Wang (2020).]{EfficientNet-B0 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.su2020}{Su and Wang (2020)}.}
  \label{fig:efficientnet-b0-architecture-adapted-from-su-and-839}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.636\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/efficientnet-b0-architecture-adapted-from-su-and-wang-2020-91.png}}
\end{figure}

EfficientNet-B0 is the baseline network of the EfficientNet family, which was found by architecture search and is enlarged by scaling depth, width and resolution together with a compound coefficient (\hyperlink{cite.tan2019}{Tan \& Le, 2019}). It is built from mobile inverted bottleneck (MBConv) blocks. Each block has a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 expansion convolution, a depthwise convolution, a squeeze-and-excitation module (\hyperlink{cite.hu2018}{Hu et al., 2018}), and a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 projection convolution, with batch normalization and the swish activation. The squeeze-and-excitation module reduces the channels to a quarter of the block input and rescales the channels of the expanded feature map. The network has a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 stem of 32 filters and 16 MBConv blocks in seven stages, followed by a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution to 1280 channels. The stages have 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4 and 1 blocks, output 16, 24, 40, 80, 112, 192 and 320 channels, use depthwise kernels of 3\ensuremath{\times}3, 3\ensuremath{\times}3, 5\ensuremath{\times}5, 3\ensuremath{\times}3, 5\ensuremath{\times}5, 5\ensuremath{\times}5 and 3\ensuremath{\times}3, and use an expansion factor of 1 in the first stage and 6 in the others. Stride-2 convolutions in stages 2, 3, 4 and 6 reduce the feature map to 3\ensuremath{\times}3, and nine blocks have skip connections.

The Keras model contains its own rescaling and normalization layers, so it received pixel values from 0 to 255. The ImageNet weights were frozen. The backbone has 4,049,571 parameters, and the dense layer adds 1,281 trainable parameters. An L2 penalty of 0.001 was applied to the weights of the dense layer.

\textbf{EfficientNet-Lite0}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[EfficientNet-Lite0 architecture. Adapted from Almutairi et al. (2022).]{EfficientNet-Lite0 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.alm2022}{Almutairi et al. (2022)}.}
  \label{fig:efficientnet-lite0-architecture-adapted-from-alm-844}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.636\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/efficientnet-lite0-architecture-adapted-from-almutairi-et-al-92.png}}
\end{figure}

EfficientNet-Lite0 is a variant of EfficientNet-B0 adapted for mobile and edge devices (\hyperlink{cite.liu2020}{Liu, 2020}). It keeps the stage layout of B0, with the same block counts, kernel sizes, strides and channel widths, and a 1280-channel final convolution. It removes the squeeze-and-excitation modules and replaces swish with ReLU6, which makes the network easier to quantize. Its stem and final convolution are also left unscaled.

This study loaded a pretrained feature-extraction version from TensorFlow Hub and kept it frozen. Its input was scaled to the range 0 to 1, and it returns a vector of 1280 values, so no additional pooling was needed. The model has 3,414,305 parameters, of which 1,281 in the dense layer were trained. An L2 penalty of 0.001 was applied to the weights of the dense layer.

\textbf{ShuffleNetV1}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[ShuffleNetV1 architecture. Adapted from Li et al. (2024).]{ShuffleNetV1 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.li2024}{Li et al. (2024)}.}
  \label{fig:shufflenetv1-architecture-adapted-from-li-et-al-850}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.636\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/shufflenetv1-architecture-adapted-from-li-et-al-2024-93.png}}
\end{figure}

ShuffleNetV1 lowers the cost of the 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolutions, which dominate the computation of efficient networks, by replacing them with group convolutions. A channel shuffle operation lets information pass between the groups (\hyperlink{cite.zhang2018}{Zhang et al., 2018}). Each unit contains a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 group convolution, a channel shuffle, a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 depthwise convolution, and a second 1\ensuremath{\times}1 group convolution, with batch normalization throughout and ReLU after the first convolution. The bottleneck width of a unit is one quarter of its output channels. In a unit with stride 1, the input is added to the output. In a unit with stride 2, a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 average pooling of the input is concatenated with the output instead. This study used 3 groups. The network has a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 stem of 24 filters followed by 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 max pooling, and then three stages of 4, 8 and 4 units with 240, 480 and 960 output channels at resolutions of 12\ensuremath{\times}12, 6\ensuremath{\times}6 and 3\ensuremath{\times}3. The first 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution of the first unit is not grouped because its input has only 24 channels.

The network was trained from scratch with the input scaled to the range \ensuremath{-}1 to 1. The backbone has 929,208 parameters, and the dense layer adds 961.

\textbf{ShuffleNetV2}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[ShuffleNetV2 architecture. Adapted from Ma et al. (2018).]{ShuffleNetV2 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.ma2018}{Ma et al. (2018)}.}
  \label{fig:shufflenetv2-architecture-adapted-from-ma-et-al-856}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.617\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/shufflenetv2-architecture-adapted-from-ma-et-al-2018-94.png}}
\end{figure}

ShuffleNetV2 was designed using practical guidelines about memory access cost and parallelism, which led to equal channel widths in the convolutions of a unit, no group convolutions, and fewer element-wise operations (\hyperlink{cite.ma2018}{Ma et al., 2018}). Each basic unit splits its input channels into two halves. One half is passed through unchanged, and the other goes through a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution, a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 depthwise convolution and a second 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution, with batch normalization and ReLU. The two halves are concatenated and a channel shuffle follows. The first unit of each stage downsamples. In this unit both branches receive the full input, the left branch applies a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 depthwise convolution and a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution, the right branch applies a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution, a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 depthwise convolution and a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution, and the branches are concatenated and shuffled. The network has the same stem as ShuffleNetV1, then three stages of 4, 8 and 4 units with 116, 232 and 464 output channels at resolutions of 12\ensuremath{\times}12, 6\ensuremath{\times}6 and 3\ensuremath{\times}3, and a final 1\ensuremath{\times}1 convolution to 1024 channels. It has no addition-based skip connections.

The network was trained from scratch with the input scaled to the range \ensuremath{-}1 to 1. The backbone has 1,269,784 parameters, and the dense layer adds 1,025.

\textbf{SqueezeNet v1.1}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \caption[SqueezeNet v1.1 architecture. Adapted from Lee et al. (2026).]{SqueezeNet v1.1 architecture. Adapted from \protect\hyperlink{cite.lee2026}{Lee et al. (2026)}.}
  \label{fig:squeezenet-v1-1-architecture-adapted-from-lee-et-862}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.971\linewidth,height=.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/squeezenet-v1-1-architecture-adapted-from-lee-et-al-2026-95.png}}
\end{figure}

SqueezeNet reduces the number of parameters by building the network from fire modules (\hyperlink{cite.iandola2016}{Iandola et al., 2016}). A fire module contains a 1\ensuremath{\times}1 squeeze convolution, followed by an expand layer in which 1\ensuremath{\times}1 and 3\ensuremath{\times}3 convolutions run in parallel and their outputs are concatenated. Version 1.1 starts with a 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 convolution of 64 filters instead of the 7\ensuremath{\times}7 convolution of 96 filters used in the original version, followed by 3\ensuremath{\times}3 stride-2 max pooling. Eight fire modules follow, with max pooling after the first pair and after the second pair. The squeeze layers have 16, 16, 32, 32, 48, 48, 64 and 64 filters, and each expand branch has 64, 64, 128, 128, 192, 192, 256 and 256 filters. The module outputs therefore have 128, 128, 256, 256, 384, 384, 512 and 512 channels, and the backbone outputs 5\ensuremath{\times}5\ensuremath{\times}512 features. All convolutions use ReLU, and the network has neither batch normalization nor skip connections.

The network was assembled layer by layer and loaded with published ImageNet weights, which were frozen. The input was converted to BGR channel order and the ImageNet channel means were subtracted, without scaling. The backbone has 722,496 parameters, and the dense layer adds 513 trainable parameters.

Hyperparameter optimization was conducted using RandomizedSearchCV with grouped 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set, where the data was shuffled with the fixed random seed. Each original recording and its two augmented copies were assigned to the same fold, so that no classifier was validated on a noisy copy of a recording it had been trained on. Instead of testing every possible combination, randomized search sampled a fixed number of hyperparameter combinations for each classifier, ranging from 60 to 150 iterations depending on the size of its search space. Classifiers with more hyperparameters to tune were given more iterations so that their larger search spaces could be explored more thoroughly: Naive Bayes, which tunes only one hyperparameter, used 60 iterations; the Support Vector Machine, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, and K-Nearest Neighbors, which tune five to six, used 100; and Random Forest and XGBoost, which tune seven and ten, used 150. Continuous hyperparameters that span several orders of magnitude, such as the regularization strength C and the SVM kernel coefficient gamma, were sampled from log-uniform distributions.

Each combination was scored using the F1-score of the ripe class, which was chosen over accuracy because ripe samples formed the minority class in the training data. Combinations not supported by the library, such as the elastic net penalty with the liblinear solver, were assigned a score of zero so that they could not be selected. The best-performing configuration for each classifier was then refit on the full augmented training set.

\subsection{Validation}

This section presents the audio machine learning model and the image deep learning architecture validation procedures conducted in this study.

\textbf{Audio Model Validation}

No separate validation set was created for the audio models. With only 395 original training recordings, of which 124 were ripe, setting aside a fixed validation set would have reduced the data available for training and left too few ripe samples for a stable performance estimate. Validation was therefore performed through 5-fold cross-validation within the training set, which allowed every training recording to contribute to both training and validation while the test set remained untouched.

Five folds were used so that each validation fold held about 79 original recordings, around 25 of them ripe, together with their augmented copies, for a total of about 237 samples per fold, which was enough to compute a meaningful F1-score while still training each model on 80\% of the training data. The folds were stratified to keep the same proportion of ripe and unripe samples in each one, since a random split could leave a fold with very few ripe samples and produce an unreliable score. The cross-validation was also grouped, with each original recording and its two noise-augmented copies always assigned to the same fold. Because the augmented copies are near-duplicates of their source recording, placing them in different folds would allow a classifier to be validated on a noisy copy of a recording it had already trained on, inflating the validation score and biasing the choice of hyperparameters. As a result, each validation fold contained both original and noisy recordings, so the validation scores reflected performance under both clean and noisy conditions.

The cross-validation results were used only for tuning and selecting the hyperparameters. Each configuration sampled during the search described in Section 3.7.2 was scored by its average F1-score for the ripe class across the five validation folds, which reduced the influence of any single split on the selection. The configuration with the highest average score was then refit on the full augmented training set, so that the final model learned from all available training data before being evaluated on the test set.

Several steps were taken to keep information from the test set out of training and validation. The recordings were split into training and test sets at the file level before any feature extraction or augmentation, and the background noise used for augmentation was drawn only from the training recordings. The scaling parameters were likewise computed from the training set only, including its augmented copies. Since the scaler was fit on the full training set before cross-validation, each validation fold had a small influence on its own scaling; this affects only the validation scores and not the final test results.

\textbf{Image Model Validation} A fixed validation set of 48 prepared photographs was used to monitor the image models during training. After each epoch, the model classified the 25 ripe and 23 unripe validation images with augmentation and dropout disabled. Validation loss and accuracy were measured with the model weights fixed. Using the same set at every epoch and across all runs provided a common basis for comparing checkpoints and optimizers.

Validation loss guided adjustments to the learning rate during training. If the loss did not improve by more than 0.0001 over the best value tracked by the learning-rate rule for four epochs, the rate was halved down to a minimum of 0.0000001. A separate stopping rule ended fitting after eight epochs without a new minimum in validation loss. The weights from the epoch with the lowest validation loss in each run were saved and restored before evaluation.

For a given architecture, the optimizer run with the lowest validation loss was retained. If two losses were equal, it used the higher validation accuracy. If both measurements were equal, the first optimizer name in alphabetical order was used. The comparison took place within each architecture, where the loss and regularization settings were the same.

Loss accounts for the scores assigned to the actual labels. Two runs can therefore have the same accuracy when they assign the same labels, yet differ in how confidently they classify those images. Recording both measurements showed how often each model was correct and how each runs with similar accuracy were distinguished.

\subsection{Testing}

This section presents the audio machine learning model and image deep learning testing procedures conducted in this study.

\textbf{Audio Model Testing}

After tuning and selection, the audio classifiers were evaluated on the held-out test set of 99 original recordings (31 ripe and 68 unripe). These recordings were excluded from training, hyperparameter tuning, augmentation, and the computation of the scaling parameters, and were used only once at this stage, so that the results would reflect performance on recordings the classifiers had never encountered. Both the default and tuned versions of all seven classifiers were evaluated on this same test set, allowing the effect of hyperparameter tuning to be compared directly.

Performance was measured using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, with the ripe class treated as the positive class. Accuracy alone was not sufficient because of the class imbalance: a classifier that labeled every recording as unripe would already reach 68.7\% accuracy on the test set while detecting no ripe watermelons at all. Precision and recall were therefore reported to show whether errors came from unripe watermelons being labeled ripe or ripe watermelons being missed, with the F1-score summarizing both. These were supported by confusion matrices, which show the exact count of each type of error, and by Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and Precision-Recall curves, which evaluate the classifiers across all decision thresholds; the Precision-Recall curves were included because they give a more informative view than ROC curves when the positive class is the minority.

Because the test set contained only 31 ripe recordings, a single misclassified ripe sample changes recall by about 3.2 percentage points. To show how much the scores could vary by chance, 95\% confidence intervals were computed for accuracy and F1-score by resampling the test set with replacement 2,000 times. Differences between classifiers that fell within these intervals were not treated as meaningful on their own.

To assess the effect of augmentation, the tuned classifiers were also evaluated on six noisy versions of the test set, containing real and brown noise at 30, 25, and 20 dB, created using background noise drawn only from the test recordings (Section 3.6.2). The results were compared with those of the same classifiers trained without augmentation, since the clean test set alone cannot show whether training on noisy copies improves robustness to noise. Lower SNRs were not tested because the thump detection itself becomes unreliable below 20 dB, as shown in the sensitivity test.

The model for deployment was selected based on its F1-score on the test set, its memory footprint, and whether it could be converted into C code for on-device inference on the ESP32-S3, since the classifier must run entirely on the microcontroller. The tuned classifiers were converted into C header files using the micromlgen library, which supported five of the seven: XGBoost could not be converted, and K-Nearest Neighbors is not supported, since it would require the entire training set to be stored on the device. The predictions of each exported model were compared against the Python model on all test samples to confirm that the conversion preserved its behavior. Each exported model was then compiled for the ESP32-S3 to confirm that it fit within the device’s memory, and the flash memory it added beyond a baseline program was measured.

The feature importances of the tree-based classifiers (Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost) were also examined to identify which features contributed most to their predictions. This was done to check whether the classifiers relied on acoustic characteristics of the thump response or on features that could reflect differences in the recording environment between the market and farm collection sites, given the difference in background noise identified in Section 3.6.2.

\textbf{Image Model Testing}

The saved checkpoints were evaluated on the 49 held-out photographs, comprising 25 ripe and 24 unripe images. These files were reserved from weight fitting and checkpoint selection. Their prepared versions used the common lighting and color reference calculated before partitioning, as described in Section 3.6.2. Every completed run was evaluated on this test set. The main comparison used one configuration per architecture, selected by validation loss.

The model assigned an unripe label when its unripe score exceeded 0.5 and a ripe label otherwise. Accuracy measured the share of correct labels. Precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated for each class and averaged equally to obtain the macro scores. This gave recognition of ripe and unripe fruit the same weight in the comparison. The confusion matrices recorded the correct classifications and the two directions of error at the classification cutoff.

The unripe scores were used to draw receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall curves at different decision cutoffs, with unripe as the positive class. ROC performance was summarized by the area under the ROC curve, and precision-recall performance was summarized by average precision.

To examine how image preparation affected the results, each prepared test photograph was matched to its raw counterpart by class and filename. The same fitted models evaluated both versions of the 49 photographs. Each version was oriented, converted to RGB, and resized to 96 \ensuremath{\times} 96 pixels at loading. Comparing scores on the same photographs isolated the change in input preparation from changes in the fitted weights or the test cases.

The eight retained configurations were converted to TensorFlow Lite with 8-bit integer inputs and outputs. A representative set of 100 prepared training images was sampled without replacement to calibrate the conversion. The saved weights were converted with augmentation and dropout disabled. Each model was exported as a TensorFlow Lite file and a C array.

Each converted model was tested on the same 49 prepared images. The input scale and zero point converted the RGB values to integers, and the output scale and zero point were used to recover the unripe score. Predictions were assigned at the same 0.5 cutoff.
