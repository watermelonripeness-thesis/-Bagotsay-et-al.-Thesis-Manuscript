\chapter{INTRODUCTION}

\begin{thesisprose}

Agriculture has historically depended on traditional practices and empirical knowledge for crop management, harvest timing, and quality assessment. These conventional methods, while functional, are inherently subjective and lack the standardization necessary for consistent and reliable evaluation, particularly in the context of fruit maturity where internal quality attributes are not directly observable (\hyperlink{cite.koc2025}{Koç \& Akbalik, 2025}). The increasing adoption of precision agriculture and data-driven sensing technologies represents a significant departure from these practices, offering objective and reproducible alternatives that are more suitable for modern agricultural quality assessment.

A critical consideration in pre-harvest management is the physiological classification of fruit crops. Non-climacteric fruits, including watermelon, citrus, grapes, and pineapple, do not undergo post-harvest ripening and therefore must attain full maturity prior to detachment from the plant. Unlike climacteric varieties such as banana and mango, which continue to ripen after harvest through ethylene-mediated processes (\hyperlink{cite.tipu2024}{Tipu \& Sherif, 2024}; \hyperlink{cite.liu2022}{Liu et al., 2022}), non-climacteric fruits exhibit no further development in sugar content, texture, or flavor once harvested. This biological characteristic makes accurate pre-harvest ripeness assessment a technical requirement rather than an optional quality measure, as premature harvest results in permanent degradation of fruit quality.

Despite this, smallholder farmers in many agricultural regions, including the Philippines, continue to rely on subjective sensory indicators such as sound resonance, surface appearance, and tactile response to determine harvest readiness. These experience-based approaches are inconsistent across individuals, lack quantifiable metrics, and frequently result in the collection of suboptimal produce. The absence of accessible and field-deployable instrumentation for objective ripeness assessment represents a technological gap that this study seeks to address.

Ripeness assessment methods can generally be divided based on two things: whether they damage the fruit, and whether they require physical contact with it. Destructive methods, like cutting or pulping the watermelon to check its internal state, give accurate results but make the fruit unsellable afterward, which is not practical for farmers or vendors. Non-destructive methods avoid this problem by keeping the fruit intact after testing. But within non-destructive methods, there is still a distinction between invasive and non-invasive approaches. Non-invasive methods do not breach or penetrate the fruit at all, such as using imaging or color analysis from a distance, or using thumping methods. Invasive non-destructive methods, on the other hand, breach the interior of the fruit, such as with using probes, but do not cause any lasting damage. The system proposed in this study falls under the non-destructive and non-invasive category, as the watermelon is not damaged in any way during the assessment.

This chapter presents the background and motivation behind the development of a portable, non-destructive watermelon ripeness detection system that integrates acoustic signal processing and machine learning. It identifies the research problem arising from the limitations of existing manual and technological assessment methods, defines the objectives of the study, and establishes the scope, significance, and limitations within which the proposed system is developed and evaluated.

\section{Background of the Study}

Advanced technologies have become a cornerstone of modern agriculture, streamlining production and quality control through data-driven methods. Recent trends emphasize the integration of machine learning to enhance efficiency through precision farming and improved resource allocation (\hyperlink{cite.boterovalencia2025}{Botero-Valencia et al., 2025}). Research indicates a paradigm shift where machine learning models have been useful in recent systems dominating over food production. These approaches allow for the evaluation of internal fruit characteristics without compromising physical integrity, aligning practices with contemporary food safety and environmental management requirements (\hyperlink{cite.araujo2023}{Araújo et al., 2023}).

The shift toward quantitative analysis is further supported by research into automated grading systems. Studies highlight how machine learning algorithms are transforming traditional agricultural practices by providing high-precision sorting and quality assurance (\hyperlink{cite.boterovalencia2025}{Botero-Valencia et al., 2025}). Furthermore, the integration of convolutional neural network and acoustic analysis has proven effective in agricultural produce handling, with specialized algorithms enabling accurate and efficient solutions for produce quality management (\hyperlink{cite.gowda2025}{Gowda et al., 2025}). These trends illustrate a growing reliance on feature extraction techniques to interpret complex biological data.

In 2023, the Philippines ranked \#52 in the global production of watermelon, having produced around 124,737.55 metric tons of watermelon (\hyperlink{cite.trends2024}{\textit{Trends of Fresh Watermelon Production in Philippines}, 2024}). Although the Philippines’ contribution to the total global production of watermelon is only around 0.12\%, it is still a high-value crop in the Philippines.

Despite these global advancements, many local farmers in the Philippines still rely heavily on traditional, manual methods for pre-harvest assessment. Currently, determining the maturity of watermelons in the field depends on the thumping technique—manually tapping the fruit and listening to the resulting sound, in addition to visual inspection and the observation of the wilting of the watermelon’s tendrils. This practice is subjective, as the interpretation of acoustic resonance varies significantly among individuals. Recent literature notes that subjective harvest decisions can lead to the collection of underripe produce, which lacks the necessary sugar content and flavor profiles required for market standards (\hyperlink{cite.koc2025}{Koç \& Akbalik, 2025}). When conducting the thumping technique, the position on the watermelon is considered inconsequential, as the sound produced travels uniformly across the entire surface of the fruit (\hyperlink{cite.nourain2004}{Nourain et al., 2004}). This reliance on human intuition creates an inconsistency in fruit quality that can affect the overall reliability of the harvest.

Acoustic-based methods have gained particular attention due to their low cost, portability, and ease of field implementation, yet their performance remains highly sensitive to environmental noise, with wind, machinery, and handling interference significantly degrading signal quality (\hyperlink{cite.pamungkas2021}{Pamungkas \& Bintoro, 2021}), and standard FFT-based feature extraction has been shown to be insufficient for capturing the subtle acoustic differences caused by internal ripeness changes (\hyperlink{cite.chen2022}{Chen et al., 2022}). Similarly, computer vision and optical-based methods are either limited to surface-level external assessment and cannot directly measure internal quality attributes such as flesh firmness or sugar content (\hyperlink{cite.perezudell2023}{Perez-Udell et al., 2023}; \hyperlink{cite.xu2024}{Xu et al., 2024}), or depend on expensive stationary equipment incompatible with portable farm-level deployment (\hyperlink{cite.qi2024}{Qi et al., 2024}; \hyperlink{cite.shah2025}{Shah et al., 2025}).

To overcome these inconsistencies, various nondestructive testing techniques have been explored, including acoustic-based methods such as FFT-based frequency analysis of tapping-induced vibration signals (\hyperlink{cite.wongkalasin2021}{Wongkalasin et al., 2021}), FFT magnitude analysis of thumping sounds using a custom mechanical apparatus (\hyperlink{cite.okafor2020}{Okafor, 2020}), acoustic impulse response measurement using mechanical tapping devices (\hyperlink{cite.pamungkas2021}{Pamungkas \& Bintoro, 2021}), and wavelet-based acoustic signal decomposition for non-destructive fruit screening (\hyperlink{cite.chen2022}{Chen et al., 2022}), as well as computer vision-based methods such as HSV color space classification from field photographs (\hyperlink{cite.perezudell2023}{Perez-Udell et al., 2023}), lightweight YOLO-based deep learning detection for real-time ripeness classification (\hyperlink{cite.xu2024}{Xu et al., 2024}), and hyperspectral imaging coupled with deep learning for non-destructive quality estimation (\hyperlink{cite.qi2024}{Qi et al., 2024}; \hyperlink{cite.shah2025}{Shah et al., 2025}).

This study looks into developing a portable system for detecting watermelon ripeness using acoustic signal processing and machine learning. The device utilizes a multi-modal approach combining a microphone to capture the acoustic signals produced by tapping the fruit in the field, and a camera module for visual feature extraction and analysis.

\section{Statement of the Problem}

Accurate and objective watermelon ripeness assessment remains a persistent challenge for pre-harvest decision-making, particularly in farming communities where ripeness assessment relies entirely on years of accumulated experience (\hyperlink{cite.tran2025}{Tran et al., 2025}). In practice, farmers in a watermelon farm in Cagayan de Oro City harvest watermelons according to their buyers' specifications, who typically request a mixed batch — a portion already fully ripe for immediate sale and a portion harvested slightly earlier, whose perceived palatability improves over the following days due to post-harvest textural and sugar redistribution rather than true biochemical ripening. This classification requires accurately distinguishing between multiple ripeness stages simultaneously, a skill that only experienced farmers reliably possess. While experienced farmers supplement this assessment with week-based tracking of growth duration, ripeness progression varies with seasonal conditions such as weather, soil quality, and irrigation, meaning that harvest timing cannot be reliably determined by growth duration alone without physical assessment of individual fruit.

For new and incoming farmers, replicating this classification without a standardized reference is difficult, as the traditional thumping technique and visual inspection of the rind provide no measurable output and cannot be easily learned or verified without extensive field experience. This knowledge gap creates inconsistency in harvest timing decisions and leaves new farmers without a practical tool to guide pre-harvest assessment.

Efforts to address this challenge have led researchers to explore nondestructive testing techniques as objective alternatives to manual assessment. Acoustic-based approaches have demonstrated that tapping-induced sound signals carry meaningful information about internal fruit quality, with frequency and magnitude patterns showing strong correlation with soluble solids content and maturity stage, yet these methods have consistently been validated only under controlled laboratory conditions rather than real open-field environments (\hyperlink{cite.wongkalasin2021}{Wongkalasin et al., 2021}; \hyperlink{cite.okafor2020}{Okafor, 2020}; \hyperlink{cite.pamungkas2021}{Pamungkas \& Bintoro, 2021}). Their performance in open field environments remains unreliable, as environmental noise from wind, machinery, and handling interference significantly degrades signal quality (\hyperlink{cite.pamungkas2021}{Pamungkas \& Bintoro, 2021}).

Computer vision-based approaches, on the other hand, are either restricted to surface-level external indicators and cannot assess internal quality attributes such as flesh firmness or sugar content (\hyperlink{cite.perezudell2023}{Perez-Udell et al., 2023}; \hyperlink{cite.xu2024}{Xu et al., 2024}), or achieve internal quality estimation only through expensive stationary hyperspectral equipment incompatible with portable farm-level deployment (\hyperlink{cite.qi2024}{Qi et al., 2024}; \hyperlink{cite.shah2025}{Shah et al., 2025}). Despite these efforts, no portable integrated system combining both acoustic and visual modalities through a unified machine learning classifier for real-time field use has been identified in the literature.

This study addresses this gap by developing a portable device that analyzes the acoustic response and rind visual characteristics of watermelons, integrating both inputs through a machine learning classifier to enable objective and real-time ripeness classification directly in the field and providing local farmers, particularly those new to the practice, with a reliable and standardized alternative to experience-based manual assessment.

\section{Objective of the Study}

This study aimed to develop a portable, non-destructive watermelon ripeness detection system that integrates acoustic signal processing and machine learning-based analysis for real-time, on-site classification. Specifically, the study aimed to accomplish the following objectives:

\begin{enumerate}

\item Design an acoustic-optical system that uses digital signal processing to extract acoustic features from the thump response of the watermelon and a camera to capture images of the watermelon rind.

\item Develop a portable, field-deployable system for real-time classification, and assess its usability.

\item Evaluate ripeness classification accuracy against cut-fruit inspection and agreement with the farmer's assessment.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Significance of the Study}

The development of a portable, non-destructive watermelon ripeness assessment system contributed to the broader goals of sustainable agricultural development, particularly the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals of Zero Hunger (SDG 2), Responsible Consumption and Production (SDG 12), and Partnerships for the Goals (SDG 17). By improving harvest timing accuracy and reducing post-harvest losses, the system directly supported food security and the promotion of sustainable agriculture aligned with SDG 2. Its capacity to minimize produce waste along the supply chain addressed SDG 12, which called for more efficient and responsible use of resources in food production and distribution. Furthermore, the methodologies and findings of this research contributed to the body of knowledge available to the wider scientific and agricultural community, supporting the technology transfer and knowledge-sharing objectives of SDG 17. The findings and output of this research were significantly beneficial to the following:

\textbf{Local Farmers and Agricultural Producers.} This study provides an objective tool that can support the traditional thumping and visual inspection methods used in ripeness assessment. By offering a measurable basis for harvest decisions, the system may help watermelon farmers reduce premature harvesting, particularly newer farmers who have not yet developed the experience needed to judge ripeness reliably.

\textbf{Classifiers}. The proposed system may assist sorters in making more consistent ripeness assessments at the farm gate, especially when sorting is done by workers with varying levels of experience. This could help ensure that watermelons are sorted according to a uniform standard before they are loaded onto trucks for distribution to other parts of the country.

\textbf{Consumers and Retailers.} Watermelons are often sold in batches that mix fully ripe fruit with fruit harvested slightly earlier, depending on where they will be sold and how long they will travel. By classifying watermelons as ripe or unripe, the system may help farmers sort these batches more consistently. This could help retailers receive produce that matches what was ordered and contribute to more consistent quality for consumers.

\textbf{Future Researchers.} This study may serve as a useful reference for developing or enhancing non-destructive ripeness assessment systems in agriculture. It offers insights into combining acoustic signal processing and image-based classification on a low-cost microcontroller for pre-harvest decision-making. It also serves as a foundation for future improvements, including adaptation to other fruits and varieties and refinement of classification accuracy and processing speed.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

The system was designed to function as a portable, handheld tool for pre-harvest watermelon ripeness assessment. It integrated acoustic signal processing and image-based analysis to evaluate both internal and external indicators of maturity, classifying watermelons into two categories: ripe and unripe. The local dataset was sourced from two locations; ripe watermelon samples were collected from Cogon Public Market in Cagayan de Oro City on July 15, 2026, where watermelons had already been harvested and confirmed ready for sale, while unripe samples were captured directly from pre-harvest fruit still attached to the vine at the Brgy. Balubal farm in Cagayan de Oro City, Northern Mindanao, on September 10, 2026. Ripe labels were based on the vendors’ confirmation that the fruits were ready for sale, while unripe labels were confirmed by the farm owner. During field testing, the system’s classifications were verified through physical cutting and inspection of each tested watermelon to confirm its internal condition. All system development and testing were conducted under natural field and market conditions to reflect actual farm use.

The system focused primarily on the Sweet 16 F1 watermelon variety, which was the principal cultivar grown at the Brgy. Balubal farm in Cagayan de Oro City. The Sweet 16 F1 variety was always planted by the farmers at Brgy. Balubal as it was a variety that was consistently demanded by their buyer. The system evaluated one watermelon at a time and was intended exclusively for pre-harvest assessment of fruit still attached to the vine. It did not support simultaneous or large-scale batch assessments, and post-harvest quality, storage behavior, and shelf life fell outside its scope.

The system was designed to operate under natural outdoor field conditions, accounting for typical variations in ambient lighting throughout the day. The image-based classification component was optimized for outdoor rind capture under normal daylight conditions, while the acoustic classification component was designed to tolerate moderate levels of background noise commonly present in farm environments, such as wind and ambient farm activity.

Results obtained from this study were specific to the Sweet 16 watermelon variety under the growing conditions of the Brgy. Balubal farm. As the system was developed and validated within a defined set of environmental and agricultural conditions, its classification performance could vary when applied to other watermelon varieties, farm settings, or geographic regions outside those covered in this study.

Furthermore, the system's operation was constrained by its onboard power configuration and embedded hardware resources. It used a dual 18650 Li-ion battery; however, during the field test, the system was limited to the use of a power bank due to hardware limitations.

The limitations of the system were primarily influenced by the capabilities of the embedded microcontroller, including processing speed, memory availability, and peripheral bandwidth, which required the deployed machine learning models to be optimized for efficient on-device inference. Additionally, the device had an estimated total weight of 0.388 kg and physical dimensions of approximately 205.23 mm \ensuremath{\times} 81.5 mm \ensuremath{\times} 165.5 mm, supporting portability but limiting the integration of larger power sources or additional hardware components. These factors defined the practical operating limits of the system in field conditions.

\section{Definition of Terms}

This section provides the key terms used in the study. These are arranged according to their relevance and importance to the research.

\textbf{Acoustic Signal Processing}. This term refers to a technique used to capture and analyze the sound waves generated from a watermelon "thump" to identify frequency patterns associated with maturity. In this study, it is applied to the resonant sound produced by thumping a watermelon to extract features associated with ripeness.

\textbf{Machine Learning.} This term refers to a category of computational algorithms that classify data by identifying patterns within extracted features. The proposed device employs it to determine watermelon ripeness from the processed visual inputs.

\textbf{Pre-harvest Assessment}. This term refers to the evaluation of fruit maturity while still attached to the vine to determine optimal harvest timing. The system is designed for field deployment to support this stage of the production cycle.

\textbf{Non-Destructive Testing.} This term refers to a quality evaluation method that inspects the internal state of a subject without causing physical damage. This principle applies to the approach of the system, as ripeness is inferred entirely through acoustic and visual analysis rather than cutting or puncturing the fruit.

\textbf{Non-Invasive Testing.} This term refers to a method of quality evaluation that requires no physical breach of the interior of the subject. The proposed system is considered non-invasive, as the thumping technique is limited to external contact and does not breach the watermelon fruit.
